Supplements for Brain Fog: What the Evidence Shows

Brain fog — that feeling of mental cloudiness, slow thinking, and poor concentration — has many possible causes. Here is what the evidence actually shows for the supplements most often suggested for it.

What Is Brain Fog, Exactly?

Brain fog is not a medical diagnosis — it is a description of symptoms: mental cloudiness, slow processing, difficulty concentrating, poor memory and mental fatigue. Brain fog is real, and it has many possible causes — poor sleep, stress, depression, medications, illness (including long COVID) and sometimes nutrient deficiencies.

That is why the root cause matters before you reach for a supplement. Fog that persists warrants a medical check to rule out thyroid problems, anemia, diabetes, sleep apnea, depression or medication side effects. Fog driven by B12 deficiency needs a completely different fix than fog driven by sleep deprivation. Below is what the evidence shows for the nutrients and supplements most often suggested.

1. Vitamin B12 — Neurological Brain Fog

B12 is essential for making myelin — the fatty sheath that insulates nerve fibers and enables fast nerve signaling. Deficiency can cause cognitive slowing, poor memory and mental fog. It is not rare: studies of vegetarians and vegans find B12 deficiency in anywhere from a few percent to over 80% of adults, especially those who don't supplement; roughly 10–30% of older adults absorb B12 from food poorly; and metformin and proton pump inhibitors both deplete it.

The tricky part: deficiency develops slowly over years as liver stores run down, and serum B12 can look normal while a functional deficiency — measured by methylmalonic acid and homocysteine — is already present. If you are vegan, vegetarian or over 50 and foggy, ask your doctor about testing. Vitamin B12 as methylcobalamin or cheaper cyanocobalamin — both work — is a sensible priority if you're at risk.

2. Omega-3 DHA — Brain Structure

DHA is the main omega-3 fat in the brain and is concentrated in nerve-cell membranes. Researchers think DHA helps keep nerve-cell membranes working well.

Trial results are mixed: omega-3 supplements haven't improved thinking in healthy older adults, though one trial in 176 young adults with low fish intake (1.16 g DHA/day for 6 months) found small gains in memory and reaction time, and people with mild cognitive impairment may benefit. Most people eating a Western diet get about 100–200 mg/day of EPA+DHA, below the 250 mg/day that European authorities suggest for adults.

3. Vitamin D — Linked, but Supplements Haven't Helped Thinking

Vitamin D receptors sit throughout the brain, including the hippocampus — a key memory area — and vitamin D influences genes involved in BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) and dopamine. These are mechanisms, not proof of benefit. A 2017 review of 29 studies found low vitamin D was linked to poorer thinking in middle-aged and older adults — but vitamin D supplements did not improve cognition in the trials. If a blood test shows you're low (below 20 ng/mL), correcting it with your doctor's guidance makes sense for your bones and general health — just don't expect it to clear brain fog on its own. Stay at or under 4,000 IU/day unless prescribed.

4. Magnesium L-Threonate — Early, Small-Trial Evidence

Magnesium helps regulate NMDA receptors and neurotransmitter release and supports the energy factories (mitochondria) in nerve cells. Most magnesium supplements cross the blood-brain barrier poorly. Magnesium L-threonate was developed to cross it, and animal studies show it raises brain magnesium where other forms do not. One small 12-week trial (44 adults aged 50–70 with memory complaints) found a magnesium L-threonate product improved an overall cognitive score; larger studies are needed. Typical products provide about 144 mg of elemental magnesium per day, under the 350 mg/day upper limit for supplemental magnesium.

5. Lion's Mane Mushroom — Promising but Small

Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus) contains hericenones and erinacines, which stimulate nerve growth factor in lab studies. A small 2009 trial (30 older adults with mild cognitive impairment in Japan) found 3 g/day of dried Lion's Mane powder improved scores on a dementia screening scale over 16 weeks; the benefit faded after stopping. Quality note: the 2009 trial used dried fruiting-body powder (about 3 g/day); products vary widely, so look for one that states the part used and the amount per serving.

6. Creatine — Mostly Under Stress

The brain burns about 20% of the body's resting energy, and creatine helps recycle ATP, the cell's energy currency, when demand is high. In a small study of 19 people, high-dose creatine (20 g/day for a week) reduced some of the drop in performance and mood after 24 hours without sleep. It may also help vegetarians, who have lower muscle creatine and showed memory gains in one small trial (brain creatine levels, however, appear similar to meat-eaters'). A 2018 review of six trials found creatine may improve short-term memory and reasoning, with mixed results for other mental skills; a 2021 review called the evidence promising but early. Dose: 3–5 g/day of creatine monohydrate is the usual daily dose; most brain studies used higher short-term doses. If you have kidney disease, check with your doctor first.

Matching the Supplement to Your Root Cause

Brain fog is not one problem — it is a symptom with many possible causes. Diet-related? If you are vegan or vegetarian, B12 comes first, and an algae-oil omega-3 is worth considering; a generally poor diet may mean broader nutrient gaps. Sleep-related? Better sleep habits come first; some people also try magnesium, or ashwagandha for stress that disrupts sleep. (Ashwagandha isn't for everyone: it has been linked to rare cases of liver injury; avoid it if pregnant or breastfeeding or if you have thyroid or liver problems, and check with your doctor if you take prescription medicines.) Age-related? Evidence is limited; small trials of magnesium L-threonate and Lion's Mane are promising, and omega-3 may help people with mild cognitive impairment. Talk to your doctor about memory changes. High mental workload? Some people use creatine and L-theanine for demanding mental work; evidence for each is modest and the combination hasn't been studied.

Safety notes. Higher-dose omega-3 can add to the effect of blood thinners such as warfarin, and doses above 1 g/day have been linked to a higher risk of atrial fibrillation in people with heart disease. Magnesium supplements aren't suitable for people with kidney disease without medical advice, and should be taken a few hours apart from some antibiotics and osteoporosis drugs (bisphosphonates).

Brain fog that persists despite good nutrition and sleep warrants medical evaluation to rule out thyroid dysfunction, anemia, diabetes, sleep apnea, depression or other treatable causes.
