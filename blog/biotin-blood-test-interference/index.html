Does Biotin Affect Blood Tests? How a Hair Vitamin Can Throw Off Your Results

Does biotin affect blood tests? It can: high-dose biotin from hair, skin and nail supplements can make some lab tests read falsely high or low, including thyroid and heart tests. Here's which tests are affected, what doses were studied, and what to tell your doctor before a blood draw.

By the Find Your Supplement team · Published November 17, 2026

The vitamin your lab didn't ask about

Biotin (vitamin B7) is in many hair, skin and nail supplements, B-complex pills and multivitamins. At the high doses common in beauty supplements, it can quietly change the numbers on your blood work.

Doctors use those numbers to judge your thyroid, or whether chest pain is a heart attack.

Why biotin trips up some tests

Many lab tests use a molecular "glue" made of biotin and a protein called streptavidin to grab the hormone or marker being measured. Extra biotin from a supplement can get in the way. Depending on how the test is built, the result can come out falsely high or falsely low (NIH ODS).

Your body isn't changing; the test is fooled. Not every test uses this design, and even some that do aren't affected.

What the studies found

In a small, preliminary 2017 study, 6 healthy adults took 10 mg of biotin a day for a week, a dose the authors called common in over-the-counter supplements. Researchers measured 11 markers, including thyroid hormones, a heart-failure marker (NT-proBNP) and vitamin D, on 37 lab tests. Biotin threw off 9 of the 23 tests that use the biotin design and none of the 14 that don't. Some read falsely high, others low. A week after people stopped biotin, every result was back to normal (Li et al., 2017).

A falsely low TSH with falsely high thyroid hormones looks like an overactive thyroid. A few case reports describe adults and children on high doses of biotin (10 to 300 mg a day, some for medical conditions) whose results suggested Graves' disease (NIH ODS). In one, the thyroid results returned to normal after biotin was stopped, far faster than real thyroid disease could resolve (Elston et al., 2016). The NIH notes that even a single 10 mg dose has interfered with thyroid tests done within 24 hours.

The most serious risk is with troponin, a blood test used to diagnose heart attacks. In a small 2019 lab study, blood from 5 volunteers taking 10 mg of biotin a day, drawn soon after a dose, was mixed with blood known to have high troponin. It made one high-sensitivity troponin T test (made by Roche) read significantly low, which the authors said could delay or miss a heart injury diagnosis. Two other troponin tests were not affected at this dose (Frame et al., 2019). The FDA reported that a patient on high-dose biotin died after a troponin test gave a falsely low result caused by biotin interference (NIH ODS).

How common is this?

At one large US medical center, it wasn't rare: in a survey of 1,944 Mayo Clinic outpatients, 7.7% said they took biotin. In blood samples from 1,442 emergency department patients there, 7.4% had biotin levels high enough to interfere with the most biotin-sensitive tests from one manufacturer (Katzman et al., 2018).

A 2019 review counted 92 suspected cases reported to the FDA since 2016. It named thyroid, reproductive hormone, heart and transplant-drug tests as the most at risk (Bowen et al., 2019).

How much biotin is too much?

The adequate daily intake for adults is about 30 mcg (35 mcg when breastfeeding), and most people get enough from eggs, fish, meat, seeds and nuts. Biotin deficiency is rare (NIH ODS). In 2019 the FDA said it was aware of many supplements containing 20 mg of biotin, and some up to 100 mg per pill, hundreds to thousands of times that amount (FDA, 2019).

No upper limit has been set, because there's no evidence biotin is toxic at high intakes. The NIH says the risk is a different kind: wrong lab results (NIH ODS). Food amounts don't typically cause it (Bowen et al., 2019).

And for hair? A 2017 review found 18 reported cases where biotin helped hair or nails, but each involved an underlying problem, such as deficiency or a rare disorder. The authors concluded there isn't enough evidence that biotin helps healthy people's hair or nails (Patel et al., 2017).

What to do before a blood test

  • Tell your doctor and the lab about every supplement you take. Check labels for biotin, vitamin B7 or vitamin H. It's also in many multivitamins, including prenatal vitamins; amounts vary widely, and the product name may not make it clear (FDA, 2019).
  • Ask your doctor whether to pause it before a planned blood test. The FDA says it isn't known whether stopping biotin for any set number of hours before a test prevents wrong results. If a doctor prescribed biotin, don't stop it without asking them.
  • In an emergency, such as chest pain, call 911 (or your local emergency number) right away, and tell the care team you take biotin. Never delay care to wait for biotin to clear.
  • If a result doesn't fit how you feel, ask whether biotin could be the reason before starting treatment. Labs can check by repeating the test after biotin has cleared, or by running it on a different testing system (Bowen et al., 2019).

The bottom line

High-dose biotin, common in hair, skin and nail supplements, can make some blood tests read falsely high or low, including thyroid and heart tests. Most people get enough biotin from food, and the evidence for hair in healthy people is lacking. Before any blood test, tell your doctor and the lab about every supplement you take, and ask whether to pause biotin first.
