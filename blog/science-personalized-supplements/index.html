The Science of Personalized Supplements: Why One Size Doesn't Fit All

Why does your friend swear by iron while it does nothing for you? Why do vegans need different supplements than omnivores? The science of nutritional individuality explains why personalization isn't optional — it's everything.

The one-size-fits-all fallacy

Walk into any supplement store and you face a wall of products. Without personalized guidance, most people do one of two things: take what an influencer recommends, or take a generic multivitamin and hope for the best. Neither works. The influencer approach fails because supplementation needs are profoundly individual — the fact that Joe Rogan takes fish oil is evidence of exactly nothing about what you need. The generic multivitamin fails because it provides inadequate doses of most nutrients while missing the specific ones you actually lack. The science is unambiguous: nutritional status varies enormously between individuals.

Diet is the primary driver

The most powerful determinant of your supplement needs is what you eat — which food groups, preparation methods, and sources. Consider iron: a person eating 500g of red meat per week absorbs heme iron at 15–35% efficiency. A vegan woman of childbearing age absorbs non-heme iron at 2–20% efficiency, from a lower baseline intake, on top of menstrual blood loss. Same nutrient, functionally different situations, completely different interventions.

The research literature consistently maps dietary patterns to specific deficiencies:

  • Vegan diets: 93–95% B12 deficiency rates in systematic reviews
  • Ketogenic diets: near-universal magnesium depletion in the first weeks
  • Standard Western diets: ~68% magnesium insufficiency and widespread omega-3 deficiency
  • Diets low in fatty fish: EPA/DHA below optimal in virtually all Western populations

Lifestyle amplifies the risk

Diet sets the baseline; lifestyle creates the amplifiers.

Sun exposure is binary for vitamin D: if you are indoors or in a northern climate most of the year, dietary vitamin D — minimal in most diets — is your only source. Two people with identical diets can have radically different vitamin D status based purely on where they live.

Exercise intensity creates specific demands. Endurance athletes lose iron through sweat, foot-strike hemolysis, and GI microbleeding; strength athletes have elevated creatine and magnesium requirements.

Chronic stress is the most underappreciated modifier: the stress response consumes magnesium, B vitamins (particularly B5 and B6), and vitamin C.

Alcohol increases urinary excretion of magnesium, zinc, and B vitamins while impairing liver B vitamin metabolism — even moderate regular use changes the picture.

Genes and age refine the picture

Roughly 40–60% of the population carries an MTHFR variant (C677T or A1298C) that reduces conversion of folic acid to active methylfolate. For these people, standard folic acid is substantially less effective — potentially counterproductive in high doses — and methylated forms of folate and B12 work better. FUT2 variants lower B12 absorption from food, and VDR variants mean some people need higher serum D3 levels for the same biological effect. These factors are real and increasingly affordable to test — but they are a refinement. Diet and lifestyle remain the primary layer.

Age matters too. After 50, stomach acid production declines in a significant share of people, and stomach acid is essential for cleaving B12 from food proteins. The Institute of Medicine therefore recommends adults over 50 meet their B12 needs primarily through supplements or fortified foods. Calcium and vitamin D absorption also decline with age, just as the consequences of deficiency — fracture risk, immune decline — become more severe. The right dose for a 70-year-old is categorically different from that for a 25-year-old.

Why standard multivitamins fail

One pill, everything covered — the appeal is understandable. The model fails on both ends: underdosing what you need and potentially overdosing what you do not. A typical multivitamin provides:

  • Vitamin D3: 400–1,000 IU, while common deficiency correction requires 2,000–4,000 IU
  • Magnesium: 50–100 mg, against an RDA of 310–420 mg — most people need 300–400 mg supplemental
  • B12: often cyanocobalamin at sub-therapeutic doses
  • Iron: sometimes therapeutic doses for everyone — problematic for men with normal iron status

Meanwhile, multivitamins almost never include omega-3s, creatine, or adequate amounts of the most commonly deficient nutrients.

The personalization framework

A rational approach to supplementation follows this hierarchy:

  • Layer 1 — Diet assessment. Map your dietary pattern to its known systematic deficiencies. Vegan? B12 and omega-3 are mandatory. Keto? Magnesium and electrolytes. Standard Western diet? Vitamin D, magnesium, and omega-3 as a baseline.
  • Layer 2 — Lifestyle risk factors. Limited sun means vitamin D risk. Intense training elevates creatine, iron, and magnesium needs. Chronic stress points to magnesium, B vitamins, and an ashwagandha consideration.
  • Layer 3 — Symptom correlation. Fatigue plus a vegan diet is B12 deficiency until proven otherwise. Poor sleep plus high stress suggests magnesium and ashwagandha. Frequent winter infections suggest vitamin D.
  • Layer 4 — Testing when appropriate. The most clinically useful blood tests for supplementation decisions: vitamin D (25(OH)D), B12/methylmalonic acid, ferritin/transferrin, and a comprehensive metabolic panel.
