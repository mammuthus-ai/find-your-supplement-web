Fish Oil vs Krill Oil: What the 2024 Network Meta-Analyses Actually Show

Krill oil typically costs several times more per gram of EPA+DHA than fish oil, and the marketing pitch is "better absorbed." We looked at the recent network meta-analyses to see whether the absorption story holds up, how the two compare on triglycerides, and what dose makes sense.

The pitch vs. the price tag

Walk through any supplement aisle and the krill oil bottle is the one with the red liquid, the prominent phospholipid-bound omega-3 claim, and a higher price — krill oil typically costs several times more per gram of EPA+DHA than fish oil. The marketing argument: krill delivers EPA and DHA attached to phospholipids rather than triglycerides, which supposedly means better absorption — so you need less, and the price evens out. What changed recently is the evidence: two network meta-analyses published in 2024 pooled enough randomized trials to compare the two — and the results are not what either side tends to claim.

Same omega-3s, different packaging

Both supplements deliver the same two active molecules — eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) — which your body uses to build resolvins and other anti-inflammatory signaling lipids. The difference is the carrier. Fish oil binds them to triglycerides (TG, re-esterified TG, or ethyl ester); krill oil binds them to phospholipids, mainly phosphatidylcholine, plus a small amount of natural astaxanthin. Phospholipids mix more easily with water, which is the theory behind the "better absorbed" claim. Whether it matters at real-world doses is the question.

Does krill oil really absorb better?

Evidence: Moderate. The 2024 network meta-analysis in Food Chemistry: X pooled 26 randomized trials. At doses under 2,000 mg of oil, krill oil's phospholipid form produced the highest blood levels (area under the curve) of any form tested, and the authors concluded that krill oil has superior bioavailability to fish oil. Higher-dose fish oil (above 3,000 mg, in re-esterified triglyceride or ethyl ester form) also raised the omega-3 index — a long-term blood marker linked to heart risk — significantly, so at higher doses both work.

A 2015 head-to-head trial in 66 adults found that when EPA+DHA doses were matched (1.3 g/day), krill and fish oil produced statistically similar plasma and red-blood-cell EPA+DHA levels over 4 weeks (krill was numerically highest in plasma; the lead author worked for an omega-3 oil maker). The takeaway: the phospholipid advantage looks real but modest. Because krill capsules usually hold less EPA+DHA and cost more, fish oil generally delivers the same amount of omega-3 at a fraction of the cost.

Triglycerides, heart outcomes, and inflammation

Evidence: Strong for lowering triglycerides, with no clear winner. The 2020 lipid-modifying network meta-analysis of 64 RCTs found no significant differences between krill and fish oil in triglycerides, LDL-C, HDL-C, or total cholesterol, and the triglyceride drop depended on dose. The 2024 cardiovascular network meta-analysis agreed: krill oil at 2,000–2,900 mg per day cut triglycerides, fish oil ethyl esters above 3,000 mg cut total cholesterol — both worked on these lipid markers (not heart attacks or strokes), and neither dominated. In the 2022 TRILOGY trial in JAMA Network Open, a prescription-grade krill product (4 g/day, about 1.2 g EPA+DHA) lowered triglycerides by about 11% more than placebo in people with very high triglycerides.

Triglyceride-lowering doses (2–4 g EPA+DHA) should be used only with your doctor. Doses above 1 g/day raise the risk of atrial fibrillation, an irregular heart rhythm.

On hard cardiovascular endpoints, honesty matters: the large heart-outcome trials (VITAL, ASCEND, STRENGTH, REDUCE-IT) used fish-derived omega-3s; most found no benefit, and the one positive trial, REDUCE-IT, used a prescription purified-EPA drug rather than over-the-counter fish oil. There is no large krill oil outcome trial either, so anyone claiming krill is proven to reduce heart attacks is going beyond the data. For inflammation, a 2025 review of mostly lab and animal studies found krill and fish oil sometimes performed similarly and sometimes one did better; whether that applies to people at real doses is unsettled.

The astaxanthin catch

Krill oil's natural astaxanthin helps it resist oxidation, and the marketing leans on it heavily. But the amount in a typical krill serving is small (check the label), while trials of astaxanthin itself have used a few milligrams a day. If astaxanthin is what you want, buy it standalone and pair it with fish oil — the European Food Safety Authority considers up to 8 mg/day from supplements safe for adults.

How to actually choose

  • Choose fish oil if you want more EPA+DHA per dollar, or if your doctor has recommended a higher dose for triglycerides. Pick an IFOS or USP certified concentrate; the IFOS program tests fish oil lots for purity and labelling accuracy.
  • Choose krill oil if fish oil gives you persistent fishy reflux, you take a low dose (under 500 mg EPA+DHA) where the phospholipid edge shows up, or cost is not a factor. Krill tends to be low in contaminants because krill are low on the food chain.
  • Dose by goal: 250–500 mg/day EPA+DHA (or two fish meals a week) for general health; 200–300 mg DHA in pregnancy (check with your prenatal provider); higher doses only with your doctor. Take it with a meal containing fat — both absorb better that way — and refrigerate after opening.

For most people, the math favors a quality fish oil concentrate: at equivalent EPA+DHA doses the effects on blood levels and lipids are essentially the same, and krill costs several times more per gram.

Caution: omega-3 supplements can interact with anticoagulants, including warfarin and direct oral anticoagulants, and may affect bleeding risk before surgery. Doses over 1 g/day raise the risk of atrial fibrillation; talk to your doctor if you have heart-rhythm problems. Consult your physician first if you take blood thinners, have a bleeding disorder, or have a fish or shellfish allergy.
