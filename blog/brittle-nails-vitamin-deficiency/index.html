Brittle, Ridged, or Peeling Nails? What Your Nails Can (and Can't) Tell You About Nutrients

Splitting, peeling, and dry nails are usually what water and dish soap do to keratin, but a few nail changes, like spoon-shaped nails, can point to a nutrient problem. This evidence-based guide covers which nail clues are worth a blood test, what the small biotin studies really show, and what no capsule will fix.

Your nails keep a slow record

Fingernails grow about 3.5 millimeters a month, so a nail takes roughly four to six months to grow out completely (Yaemsiri et al., 2010). That makes each one a slow-motion record of recent months. Splitting, peeling, or ridges are sometimes the visible edge of a health problem, but far more often they are simply what water, dish soap, and nail-polish remover do to keratin. The useful skill is telling those apart.

This guide covers the nail changes that genuinely point to a nutrient problem, what the small biotin studies really show, and the honest list of causes no capsule will fix.

Iron: when nails turn thin or spoon-shaped

Iron deficiency is the best-established link between a nutrient and a specific nail shape. Beyond generic brittleness, it can produce koilonychia: nails that thin and scoop upward like tiny spoons, along with fatigue and pale skin (Ahmad et al., 2026). Spoon nails are a clue, not proof. They can also be normal in young children and have other causes, including iron overload (AAFP).

That is why spooning or persistent softness earns a ferritin blood test, not a guess. Ferritin shows how much iron you have stored (MedlinePlus). Don't take iron blind: excess iron is harmful, and very high doses can be fatal to children (NHS). If your ferritin comes back low, your doctor should also find out why. In women who have periods, heavy periods are a common reason, but in men and post-menopausal women low iron can mean hidden gut bleeding that needs checking (AAFP). If your doctor recommends iron, some people find a gentler form such as ferrous bisglycinate easier on the stomach; take it at the dose your doctor recommends, away from coffee and tea, and expect a retest in about 8 to 12 weeks.

Biotin: small but nail-specific studies

Biotin is a B vitamin your body uses to turn food into energy. Real biotin deficiency is rare, but it can cause brittle nails, and biotin is the rare beauty-aisle supplement studied specifically in nails. In one small scanning electron microscopy study, eight people with brittle, splitting nails who took 2.5 mg of biotin daily for several months had nails about 25 percent thicker afterward (Colombo et al., 1990). A later look back at 35 patients' records found 22 (63 percent) reported improvement (Hochman et al., 1993). The honest caveat: these studies are small, old, and had no placebo group. A 2018 dermatology review called the results promising but said larger controlled trials are still needed (Lipner and Scher, 2018), and a 2024 review rated the evidence weak (Curtis and Lipner, 2024).

If you try it, the studies used 2,500 mcg daily and judged results after roughly 2 to 10 months, so give it about six months, because nails are slow. One important warning: high-dose biotin can throw off some lab tests, including thyroid tests and a troponin test used to diagnose heart attacks. Tell your doctor and the lab that you take it, and ask whether to pause it before bloodwork (FDA; Curtis and Lipner, 2024).

White spots, grooves, and soft nails

Small white spots are almost always from minor bumps to the nail root, not a deficiency, and they grow out on their own. Horizontal grooves (Beau's lines) mark a pause in nail growth, usually after a fever, illness, or injury weeks or months earlier (Cleveland Clinic). Real zinc deficiency is uncommon and tends to bring other signs, like slow wound healing or hair shedding. People at higher risk include vegetarians and vegans, heavy drinkers, and anyone with gut disease (NIH ODS). If that is you, start with food (shellfish, meat, pumpkin seeds, legumes) or ask your doctor about a short course, and remember the 40 mg daily limit counts food and supplements together. Severe protein shortfalls from crash dieting or serious illness can also show up in nails, but a normal diet is rarely the problem.

What no supplement will fix

Most brittle nails are made, not fed. Constant wet-dry cycling (dishes, hand-washing), acetone removers, gel manicure cycles, winter air, and plain aging all crack keratin from the outside, and the fix is gloves, moisturizer on the nails and cuticles, and a lighter polish schedule, not capsules.

See a doctor rather than a supplement aisle if a nail change is new and unexplained: spooning (possible anemia), clubbing (lung or heart disease), a new or widening dark streak, especially if the color spreads onto the skin, which can be melanoma (AAD), pitting (psoriasis), thick yellow crumbly nails (fungus), or nails lifting off the bed. Those need a check now, not a six-month experiment.

The bottom line

Read the clue before buying the fix. Splitting and peeling with no other symptoms: start with gloves and moisturizer, and if you try biotin, know the studies are small and give it six months. Spooned or thin nails: test ferritin first, treat iron only if it is low, and find out why. White spots and grooves: usually old bumps or a past illness that grow out.
