Itchy Skin But No Rash? The Nutrient Tests Worth Asking About

An itch that crawls under your skin with no rash is easy to dismiss, but it is sometimes linked to a measurable nutrient gap, and low iron is the best-documented one. This guide covers which blood tests to ask your doctor about, what the evidence does and doesn't show, and how a confirmed deficiency is usually treated.

The itch with nothing to show for it

A prickling, crawling itch under the skin — no rash, no bite, no hives, and moisturizer barely helps. It sounds imaginary; it is not. Itching on normal-looking skin is a recognized medical presentation. Dry skin is a common reason, but the Mayo Clinic also lists internal conditions — including anemia, diabetes, and thyroid, kidney, and liver problems — among the causes (Mayo Clinic).

Itching all over your body is worth a doctor visit on its own (NHS). When a nutrient is involved, low iron is the best-documented one. Low B12 is less common and mostly causes tingling rather than true itch. Both show up on routine blood tests your doctor can order.

Iron: the best-documented nutrient link

Doctors have reported whole-body itching as a sign of iron deficiency since the 1970s, mostly in single-patient case reports (Lewiecki & Rahman, 1976; Valsecchi & Cainelli, 1983). In a 2021 study of 200 patients in India with body-wide itching and no visible skin lesions, 29% had serum ferritin below 15 µg/L — outright iron deficiency (Saini et al., 2021). That study had no comparison group, and most of those patients were also anemic, so it shows how common low iron was in this group, not that it caused the itch.

You do not need to be anemic to run low: ferritin (storage iron) drops first, while your blood count can still look normal, so ask for ferritin specifically (NIH ODS). Other signs include tiredness, hair loss, nail changes, restless legs, and sometimes itching (NHS). At risk: heavy periods, pregnancy, frequent blood donors, vegetarians and vegans, and gut conditions such as celiac or Crohn's disease (Mayo Clinic).

If a test confirms it — with one hard rule. Don't start iron without a test. Excess iron is harmful, and low iron needs its cause found, so ask your doctor why you're low. In one large trial of blood donors with low iron stores, 7% of those taking 30 mg of iron as ferrous bisglycinate every other day were iron deficient after eight weeks, compared with 49% on a matching placebo (Karregat et al., 2025). Your doctor may choose a different dose or form, such as ferrous bisglycinate, and will usually retest within a few months.

Vitamin B12: nerves, not skin

Low B12 doesn't usually cause true itch. It damages nerves, which causes tingling, pins and needles, or numbness, often in the hands and feet — and some people describe that as crawling or itching. This one has a deadline: nerve problems can start before any anemia shows and can sometimes be permanent, so get tested soon (NHS). Get tested before taking B12 pills, because they can make the test look normal (NICE).

At risk: vegans, adults over 50, people on metformin or acid blockers, and people with pernicious anemia or past stomach or bowel surgery. If a test shows low B12, high-dose oral B12 (1 to 2 mg a day) worked as well as shots in trials. Shots are usually preferred if the deficiency or nerve symptoms are severe, or if you have an absorption problem (AAFP).

Vitamin D, zinc, and omega-3s: weaker evidence

These three often get recommended for itchy skin, but the research comes from rashes, not itch without one.

  • Vitamin D: people with chronic hives (a rash) tend to have lower levels, and small trials suggest supplements may help as an add-on when levels are low, mostly at higher doses than usual (Siddiqui et al., 2025). There's no evidence it helps itch without a rash.
  • Zinc: low zinc has been linked to eczema, but the studies are weak, and zinc pills haven't been shown to stop itch (Gray et al., 2019). Only take zinc if a doctor finds a deficiency, and keep your total at or below 40 mg a day including food (NIH ODS).
  • Omega-3s: a deficiency bad enough to affect skin is uncommon, since most US adults get enough omega-3s from food (NIH ODS), and fish oil hasn't been shown to stop itch. Eating oily fish twice a week is still a good habit.

When to see a doctor sooner

Go soon if the itch keeps you up at night, started with a new medication or supplement, or comes with yellow skin or eyes, weight loss, fever, or night sweats. Itching all over can be a sign of thyroid, kidney, or liver disease, and less often of other illnesses (Mayo Clinic). Pregnant and itchy? Tell your midwife or doctor. If the itch is intense, worse at night, or on your palms or soles, call your maternity unit now, because it can be a liver condition that needs urgent treatment (NHS).

The bottom line

If the itch covers your whole body, keeps you up, or comes with any of the symptoms above, book a doctor visit soon rather than waiting it out. Otherwise, give basic skin care two weeks, then see your doctor and ask for ferritin and B12 to be checked along with the usual tests.
