Top Supplements for Sleep: What the Science Actually Shows

About one in three US adults regularly sleep less than 7 hours. Here is what clinical trials actually show for popular sleep supplements (modest effects at best), and why CBT-I comes first for chronic insomnia.

The sleep crisis

About one in three US adults regularly sleep less than 7 hours (CDC). Chronic short sleep is linked to impaired glucose metabolism, immune changes, cognitive problems, and higher cardiovascular risk.

Most sleep supplement advice swings between heavy pharmaceutical sedation and ineffective wellness-aisle products. The evidence-based middle ground — supplements with modest but real evidence — is smaller than the marketing suggests. Here are five popular options and what the trials actually found.

First, a note on chronic insomnia: for insomnia lasting 3 months or more, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the recommended first treatment, ahead of any pill or supplement. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine suggests against using melatonin or valerian for chronic insomnia (AASM, 2017).

Magnesium: a reasonable first try for some people

Magnesium is a reasonable first try for some people, though the evidence is limited, mostly small studies in older adults. Magnesium interacts with calming brain signals (GABA and NMDA), and one trial in older adults found higher melatonin and lower cortisol.

A small 2012 trial in 46 older adults with insomnia found that magnesium (500 mg as magnesium oxide, above the usual supplement limit and given under study supervision) improved self-reported sleep efficiency and time to fall asleep, but not total sleep time (Abbasi et al., 2012). A 2021 review rated the evidence low quality (Mah and Pitre, 2021).

Form: magnesium glycinate is gentle on the stomach and a popular choice. Citrate is also well absorbed, and the sleep trials mostly used other forms. Pick a product that gives no more than 350 mg of elemental magnesium a day. View on Amazon

Dose: up to 350 mg of elemental magnesium a day from supplements (check the "elemental magnesium" line on the label), 30–60 minutes before bed. Avoid if you have kidney disease unless your doctor approves.

Melatonin: the circadian signal

Melatonin is mainly a timing signal rather than a strong sedative. It tells your brain it is night, so it is best suited to circadian disruption — jet lag, shift work, delayed sleep phase — and possibly age-related sleep problems, since production tends to decline with age.

The most common mistake is dosing: the 5–10 mg pills in most US stores are roughly 10–30 times the 0.3 mg dose shown to restore normal night-time levels in older adults (Zhdanova et al., 2001). Studies show 0.3–0.5 mg shifts the body clock about as effectively as higher doses, with fewer next-day side effects (Burgess et al., 2010). For shifting your body clock, timing matters: low doses work best taken several hours before your usual bedtime. A 2013 meta-analysis of 19 trials found melatonin helped people fall asleep about 7 minutes faster and sleep about 8 minutes longer — modest effects (Ferracioli-Oda et al., 2013). View on Amazon

Dose: 0.3–1 mg, 30–60 minutes before target bedtime (or several hours earlier to shift your body clock). Start low. Choose a third-party-tested (for example USP-verified) product, since gummies are often mislabeled, and keep it away from children. Check with a clinician if you're pregnant, have epilepsy or take blood thinners (NCCIH). For ongoing insomnia, see a clinician about CBT-I.

Ashwagandha (KSM-66): for stress-related sleeplessness

Stress and poor sleep feed each other, and ashwagandha may help some people. A small (60-person) 2019 RCT in people with insomnia — 300 mg of KSM-66 extract twice daily for 10 weeks — showed improvements in sleep onset latency (about 29 vs 34 minutes on placebo), sleep quality, sleep efficiency, mental alertness on rising, and anxiety scores (Langade et al., 2019). A second trial (2021, 80 adults) found improvements in sleep, especially in people with insomnia (Langade et al., 2021), and a 2021 meta-analysis found effects were small overall and larger at 600 mg/day or more (Cheah et al., 2021). A mouse study found a leaf compound (triethylene glycol) promoted sleep; this hasn't been tested in people. View on Amazon

Dose: 300 mg twice daily (as studied) for up to 3 months. Rare liver injury has been reported; stop and see a doctor if you notice yellow skin or eyes, dark urine or abdominal pain. Avoid if pregnant or breastfeeding, or if you have a thyroid or autoimmune condition or hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, and check with a clinician if you take sedatives, thyroid, diabetes or blood-pressure medicines (NCCIH).

L-theanine: for racing thoughts

L-theanine, an amino acid found mainly in tea leaves, may promote a relaxed-but-alert brain-wave pattern (small studies) without sedation. Its main appeal is easing the mental chatter that keeps some people lying awake. A 2011 trial in 98 boys with ADHD (400 mg/day) found better sleep efficiency but no change in time to fall asleep (Lyon et al., 2011). A small 2019 trial in 30 healthy adults (200 mg/day) found better self-rated sleep quality (Hidese et al., 2019). View on Amazon

Dose: 200–400 mg, 30–60 minutes before bed.

Glycine: the underrated amino acid

Glycine may help by slightly lowering core body temperature, which normally falls as you fall asleep. It also acts as an inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brainstem. A small 2012 study found 3 g glycine at bedtime reduced next-day fatigue and improved alertness in healthy people who were sleep-restricted (Bannai et al., 2012). Magnesium glycinate already supplies some glycine, though less than the studied dose.

Dose: 3 g before bed.

Matching options to the problem

  • Cannot fall asleep (stress or anxiety driven): magnesium, ashwagandha or L-theanine
  • Circadian disruption (shift work, jet lag): low-dose melatonin (0.3–0.5 mg) + light therapy
  • Short sleep and tired the next day: glycine (small studies)
  • Waking during the night: no supplement has good evidence here; cutting back on late alcohol and caffeine, ruling out sleep apnea, and CBT-I are better first steps
  • Age-related sleep decline: melatonin (0.3–1 mg) and/or magnesium

Don't combine sleep supplements with alcohol, sleeping pills or other sedatives without medical advice (NCCIH).

Weaker or overhyped options

  • Valerian root: evidence is inconsistent. Some reviews show small improvements in self-rated sleep quality (Shinjyo et al., 2020), but there's no convincing evidence it treats insomnia (Valente et al., 2024), and AASM advises against it for chronic insomnia.
  • CBD: human RCT data is thin. Some anxiolytic effect appears at 300–600 mg, but sleep-specific evidence remains preliminary and inconsistent. CBD can interact with many medicines.
  • High-dose melatonin (5–10 mg): no better for most people and more likely to leave melatonin levels raised into the morning, causing grogginess.

Chronic insomnia can have underlying medical causes, such as sleep apnea — see a healthcare provider if problems persist; supplements do not replace medical treatment.
