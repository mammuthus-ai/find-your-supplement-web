Natural Supplements for Anxiety: What the Science Actually Says

Looking for natural supplements for anxiety? This evidence-based guide grades six popular options — ashwagandha, lavender (Silexan), L-theanine, magnesium, passionflower and omega-3s — by the strength of the research, with the doses studied and the safety points to know.

The truth about natural anxiety supplements

Search for natural supplements for anxiety and you will find chaos: every wellness brand promises to melt stress or restore calm. Meanwhile, many people genuinely want non-prescription options — to avoid medication side effects, to complement existing treatment, or simply to see what the research says.

Start with what works best. For anxiety disorders, the first-line treatments are psychotherapy — cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is considered the "gold standard" — and, when needed, medications such as SSRIs or SNRIs (NIMH). Supplements are at most an add-on, not a replacement.

The good news: a few supplements have reasonable clinical evidence. The bad news: that evidence varies a lot, and much of it comes from small or manufacturer-funded trials. Below are six of the most studied options, graded simply — Strong means multiple high-quality RCTs or meta-analyses with consistent findings (none of these reach it yet); Moderate means several generally positive RCTs plus a provisional guideline recommendation; Limited means small, mixed or early studies — with the doses that were studied.

Ashwagandha: the most studied herb

Ashwagandha is the most studied adaptogen for anxiety. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis — nine randomized controlled trials, 558 participants — found significant reductions in perceived stress, anxiety symptoms, and cortisol; long-term safety is unknown. An earlier 2019 double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 60 stressed adults found a 240 mg extract (Shoden) cut anxiety scores by 41% (versus 24% on placebo) and morning cortisol by 23% (versus no change on placebo). Ashwagandha is one of only a few herbals (with lavender/Silexan) given a provisional recommendation for anxiety disorders by WFSBP and CANMAT (2022).

Dose: 300–600 mg per day of root extract standardized to at least 5% withanolides, with food, for about 8 weeks. Studied branded extracts include KSM-66, Sensoril and Shoden; skip generic unstandardized powders.

Safety: Don't use ashwagandha if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, have a thyroid or autoimmune condition, or have hormone-sensitive prostate cancer. Stop and see a doctor if you notice dark urine, yellow skin or eyes, or itching (rare liver injury). Check with your prescriber if you take thyroid, diabetes, blood-pressure, immune-suppressing, seizure or sedative medicines. Long-term safety beyond about 3 months is unknown (NCCIH).

Lavender oil (Silexan): a promising option

Silexan is an oral capsule of standardized lavender oil — not aromatherapy, whose evidence is much weaker. In a 77-patient trial funded by the maker, 80 mg Silexan worked about as well as a low dose (0.5 mg) of lorazepam over 6 weeks, without sedation. A manufacturer-supported 2023 meta-analysis of 5 placebo-controlled trials (1,213 people) found it beat placebo on anxiety scores, with side effects no different from placebo.

Dose: 80 mg once daily; trials judged results at 6–10 weeks. In the US it is sold as Calm Aid.

L-theanine: limited evidence for anxiety

This green tea amino acid is popular for everyday stress, but the anxiety evidence is thin. In a small 30-person trial (supplied by the manufacturer), 200 mg/day improved some sleep measures versus placebo; anxiety scores fell, but not clearly more than with placebo. A 2019 RCT found adjunctive L-theanine (450–900 mg per day) did not beat placebo on anxiety scores but improved sleep satisfaction in GAD patients already on antidepressants.

Dose: Trials used 200 mg daily (up to 900 mg in the GAD trial); Suntheanine is the most studied branded form.

Magnesium: worth checking your intake

Many Americans get less magnesium from food than recommended, though true deficiency is uncommon in healthy people (NIH ODS). Magnesium blocks NMDA receptors — a proposed calming mechanism, not proof of benefit. In a 2024 systematic review in Cureus, 5 of 7 small studies reported less anxiety; the authors say larger trials are needed and benefit may be greatest if your magnesium is low. Use a well-absorbed form such as citrate or glycinate; avoid oxide.

Dose: 200–350 mg elemental magnesium daily; avoid if you have kidney disease, and separate it from some antibiotics and osteoporosis drugs by a few hours.

Passionflower and omega-3s: early evidence

Three small trials (one a 36-person pilot, one using a multi-herb product) suggest passionflower may ease anxiety — promising but preliminary (systematic review). Follow the label of a standardized product; trials used different preparations. It can cause drowsiness, so avoid combining it with sedatives or alcohol. For omega-3s, some trials suggest about 2 g/day EPA+DHA may modestly reduce anxiety (meta-analysis); check with your doctor if you take blood thinners. Doses above 1 g/day have also been linked to a higher risk of atrial fibrillation, an irregular heartbeat (meta-analysis).

What to skip

Valerian shows mixed results for anxiety (its evidence is better for sleep). Kava carries documented liver toxicity concerns and failed a recent well-designed trial. CBD evidence is preliminary at best. Oral GABA barely crosses the blood-brain barrier, so effects are likely minimal. Skip anything whose case rests on mechanism alone.

Combining safely

For daytime anxiety, some people pair ashwagandha in the morning with L-theanine as needed. For anxiety that disrupts sleep, others use ashwagandha in the morning and magnesium or Silexan in the evening. One hard rule: ashwagandha, passionflower and lavender can add to the effect of sedatives (benzodiazepines, sleep aids, alcohol); ashwagandha also interacts with thyroid, diabetes, blood-pressure and immune-suppressing drugs. If you take an SSRI or any other prescription medicine, or are pregnant or breastfeeding, tell your doctor before combining anything.

The bottom line

Therapy (especially CBT) and, if needed, medication are the proven first steps for an anxiety disorder. If you want to try a supplement alongside that, ashwagandha and Silexan have the most research, L-theanine and magnesium are low-risk but less proven, and each needs several weeks before you judge it.
