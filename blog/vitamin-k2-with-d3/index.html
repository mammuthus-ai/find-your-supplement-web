Should You Take Vitamin K2 With D3? What the Trials Actually Show

Should you take vitamin K2 with D3? The pairing makes biological sense, but trials haven't shown that adding K2 to D3 means fewer fractures or heart attacks. Here's what the studies found, who should skip K2, and when D3 on its own is enough.

By the Find Your Supplement team · Published November 6, 2026

Why so many D3 bottles come with K2

D3 is often sold with K2, promising that K2 "sends calcium to your bones, not your arteries."

The idea comes from real biology. Vitamin D helps your body absorb calcium (NIH ODS). Vitamin K is needed to make proteins that handle calcium, including osteocalcin in bone and matrix Gla protein, found in blood vessel walls, which may help limit calcium build-up there (NIH ODS). But do trials show a difference that matters?

What K2 did for bones

In a 3-year Dutch trial, 244 healthy postmenopausal women took 180 mcg of K2 (as MK-7) or a placebo daily. The K2 group lost less bone density at the lower spine and the hip (femoral neck), though not at the total hip (Knapen et al., 2013). It was too small to tell whether K2 prevents fractures.

A 1-year Norwegian trial in 334 healthy women aged 50 to 60, taking 360 mcg of MK-7 daily, found no difference in bone loss at any site (Emaus et al., 2010).

What about adding K2 to D3? In a 1-year Danish trial, 148 postmenopausal women with low bone density all took calcium and vitamin D, plus 375 mcg of MK-7 or a placebo. Adding K2 made no difference to bone density at any site, though one measure of bone structure in the shin held up better (Rønn et al., 2016).

In Japan, a far larger dose of another form, MK-4 (45 mg a day), is a prescription osteoporosis drug. A 2019 review of trials in people with osteoporosis found it may slightly improve spine bone density, but its effect on fractures was uncertain, and side effects were more common than with placebo (Su et al., 2019).

What K2 did for arteries

These trials measured calcium on heart scans, not heart attacks.

The AVADEC trial gave 365 Danish men (average age 71) with calcium in a heart valve 720 mcg of MK-7 plus 1,000 IU (25 mcg) of vitamin D, or placebo, for 2 years. It did not slow valve calcification, and calcium in the heart arteries and aorta rose about the same in both groups (Diederichsen et al., 2022).

Two 2026 trials were more positive. In DANCODE, 398 people (median age 71) with severe calcium build-up in their heart arteries took the same doses or placebo for 2 years. Calcium scores rose less with K2 and D3 (196 vs 248 points), while total artery plaque grew similarly in those who had a detailed scan (Hasific et al., 2026). In a Dutch trial of 180 people with heart symptoms and coronary calcium, 2 years of MK-7 alone also appeared to slow calcium build-up compared with placebo (Vossen et al., 2026).

None compared K2 plus D3 against D3 alone, and no trial has shown that K2 prevents heart attacks or strokes.

Is D3 without K2 risky?

Some worry that D3 alone "pushes calcium into your arteries." In a 3-year trial of 311 adults aged 55 to 70 who weren't low in vitamin D, 400, 4,000 or 10,000 IU of D3 a day made no difference to calcium build-up in leg arteries (Billington et al., 2020). That's not a reason to take high doses.

The real risk is too much vitamin D, which can raise blood calcium, causing nausea, vomiting, muscle weakness, confusion and kidney stones. The adult upper limit is 4,000 IU (100 mcg) a day from all sources unless your doctor prescribes more (NIH ODS). Adding K2 doesn't make megadoses safe.

Who should skip K2, or ask first

If you take warfarin (Coumadin) or another vitamin K antagonist, don't start, stop or change vitamin K, including D3 + K2 combos, without talking to your doctor. Sudden changes in vitamin K intake can change how well these blood thinners work (NIH ODS).

Otherwise, no upper limit has been set for vitamin K, which has a low potential for toxicity; no harm has been reported from vitamin K in food or supplements. Adults need 90 mcg (women) to 120 mcg (men) a day, and most people in the US get enough from food, mostly leafy greens (K1). K2 is found in natto, and in smaller amounts in cheese, eggs and meat (NIH ODS). If you have kidney disease, osteoporosis or a heart condition, ask your doctor first, and don't use supplements in place of prescribed treatment.

If you still want to try it

If your goal is vitamin D, plain vitamin D3 is enough. The recommended amount for adults is 600 IU a day (800 IU over 70), from all sources combined (NIH ODS). For postmenopausal women wanting to try K2 for bones, 180 mcg of MK-7 a day is the dose from the 3-year trial that found slower bone loss (Knapen et al., 2013), though a 1-year trial at twice that dose found no effect. It's sold alone or in a D3 + K2 (MK-7) softgel. Take it with a meal containing some fat, and keep your total D3 at or under 4,000 IU.

The bottom line

Pairing K2 with D3 appears safe for most people (warfarin aside), but trials haven't shown that it prevents fractures or heart attacks, or that it beats D3 alone for bone density. K2 had mixed results for bone loss and for calcium build-up on heart scans. D3 alone is fine at normal doses. On warfarin? Don't add K2 without your doctor.
