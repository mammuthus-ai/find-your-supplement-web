Supplements for Runners Over 40: An Evidence-Graded Guide

Running after 40 brings slower recovery, joint wear, and gradual muscle loss. This evidence-graded guide covers which supplements may be worth considering for runners over 40, what the studies actually tested, safe doses, and what to skip.

Why Running After 40 Is a Different Game

You still lace up. You still push. But the recovery that used to take one day now takes two, your knees talk back on long runs, and a minor injury lingers for weeks instead of days. This is not weakness — it is physiology:

  • Muscle loss (sarcopenia): adults gradually lose muscle mass from middle age onward, and the loss speeds up after about 60 (Volpi et al., 2004). Less muscle means less power and more injury risk.
  • Slower recovery: older muscle responds less strongly to protein, so it can take more protein to get the same repair signal after hard sessions.
  • Joint wear and falling bone density: decades of cartilage stress plus hormone-driven drops in bone mineral density raise stress fracture risk.
  • Changing nutrient status: with age, magnesium status tends to fall and your skin makes less vitamin D from sunlight.

Food, sleep, and smart training come first. Supplements can fill specific gaps. Here are seven supplements with at least some evidence that may be relevant to runners over 40 — with honest notes on who was actually studied.

The foundation: vitamin D3, protein, electrolytes

Vitamin D3 (evidence: moderate, mainly if you are low). Stress fractures are among the most frustrating running injuries. In a large randomized trial of 5,201 female Navy recruits, calcium plus 800 IU vitamin D daily cut stress fractures by about 20% (6.6% to 5.3%) during basic training (Lappe et al., 2008). Whether this applies to masters runners hasn't been tested. Some masters runners, especially those who train indoors or live far from the equator, have low vitamin D without knowing it. If you don't get much sun, 600–2,000 IU of D3 daily is a common, safe range, taken with a meal that contains fat. Don't exceed 4,000 IU/day from all sources unless your doctor prescribes it. If your doctor tests you, 20 ng/mL or above is considered adequate for most people; above 50 ng/mL is considered too high. Check with your doctor first if you take a thiazide diuretic, steroids, statins or orlistat.

Protein (evidence: strong). Older muscle needs more protein per meal to get the same muscle-building response. In one study, men around 71 needed about 0.40 g/kg of protein per meal to max out muscle protein synthesis, versus 0.24 g/kg in men around 22 (Moore et al., 2015). That study didn't test middle-aged runners, but aiming for 30–40 g of protein after hard sessions is a reasonable target. Aim for about 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day in total (roughly 100–140 g for a 70 kg runner), per the ISSN position stand. If you have kidney disease, talk to your doctor first. Whey is convenient after workouts; casein before bed can support overnight muscle repair; pea and rice blends work for total intake.

Electrolytes (evidence: moderate). Thirst becomes less reliable as we age, especially after 65, so you can be meaningfully dehydrated before you feel it. Sweat sodium varies a lot from person to person (Montain et al., 2007); "salty sweaters" — white residue on your clothes — lose much more than average. Over-drinking plain water on long efforts can dangerously dilute blood sodium. On runs over 75 minutes, take 200–400 mg sodium every 45–60 minutes.

Possible additions: magnesium, omega-3s, creatine

Magnesium glycinate (evidence: limited). Magnesium is a cofactor in over 300 enzyme systems. It is lost in sweat only in small amounts, and most people can meet their needs from food (leafy greens, nuts, seeds, whole grains). A 2022 large cohort study found that higher magnesium intake was linked to a lower chance of short sleep (under seven hours), though this kind of study can't prove magnesium caused it. If your intake is low, take 200–350 mg elemental magnesium as glycinate or citrate in the evening; don't exceed 350 mg/day from supplements. Skip poorly absorbed oxide. Avoid magnesium supplements if you have kidney disease unless your doctor advises them.

Omega-3 fish oil (evidence: limited). At 45 the inflammatory cascade after a long run may resolve more slowly than at 25. A 2024 systematic review of RCTs found lower muscle-damage and inflammatory markers, including IL-6, in some studies, with mixed effects on soreness — though the authors called the evidence inconclusive and couldn't recommend a dose. If you try fish oil, 1,000–2,000 mg EPA+DHA daily is a common dose — check the EPA+DHA content, not the total fish oil weight. Doses above 1 g/day slightly raise the risk of atrial fibrillation (Gencer et al., 2021), so check with your doctor if you have heart rhythm problems or take blood thinners such as warfarin.

Creatine monohydrate (evidence: strong for strength training; limited for running). The ISSN position stand calls creatine the most researched and effective ergogenic supplement available, with a robust safety record in healthy people. In older adults who lifted weights 2–3 times a week, creatine plus resistance training produced 1.37 kg more lean tissue than training alone (Chilibeck et al., 2017). A 2023 narrative review suggests creatine may help with surges and finishing sprints, but results in endurance races are mixed, and the 1–2 kg of water weight can work against runners. If you also strength train, take 3–5 g of monohydrate daily; no loading phase needed, and no reason to pay for fancier forms. If you have kidney disease, don't take creatine without your doctor's OK.

If joints complain: collagen peptides

Knee and joint pain is a common reason older runners cut back. A 2025 trial in 80 adults with early knee osteoarthritis found 3 g/day of low-molecular-weight collagen peptides reduced knee pain over six months (the study had industry co-authors, and it did not study runners). Larger 10–15 g doses taken with vitamin C before exercise have only been tested on lab markers in very small studies, so if you try collagen, the 3 g/day dose is the one with pain data behind it. See a physiotherapist or doctor for persistent joint pain — strength work and load management usually matter more than any supplement.

What to skip

Glucosamine and chondroitin failed to beat placebo for knee pain overall in the NIH-funded GAIT trial (except possibly in a moderate-to-severe pain subgroup); collagen has some early, small-trial evidence. BCAAs are redundant if you hit total protein targets. Proprietary recovery blends package basic ingredients at premium prices — the standalone supplements above are almost always more cost-effective.

How to phase them in

Start with the basics: check whether you're low on vitamin D (sun, diet, and your doctor's advice), audit your protein intake, and use electrolytes on long runs. Consider magnesium, omega-3s, or creatine next only if they fit a specific gap or goal. Try collagen if knees complain, alongside a physio's plan.
