Ashwagandha vs Rhodiola for Stress: How They Compare (2026 Evidence Review)

Ashwagandha and Rhodiola rosea are two of the most popular adaptogens for stress, and they are often treated as interchangeable. We look at what the meta-analyses and trials actually show, the doses studied, the safety cautions, and which one may suit anxious stress versus fatigue.

Two adaptogens, two different stress problems

Walk into any supplement store and ask for something for stress, and you will almost certainly be handed ashwagandha or Rhodiola rosea. They are two of the most popular adaptogens, both thought to act on the body's stress-hormone system (the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal, or HPA, axis), and they are routinely lumped together as if interchangeable.

The research suggests they may suit different situations. Ashwagandha is usually described as calming — it has been studied mostly for perceived stress, anxiety and sleep, so it is the more logical fit when stress shows up as poor sleep, anxiety, and feeling wired-but-tired. Rhodiola is usually described as energizing — it has been studied mostly for fatigue and mental performance, so it is the more logical fit when stress shows up as burnout and brain fog. Neither has strong evidence, and neither replaces sleep, exercise, or proven treatments such as therapy (for example CBT) for anxiety or depression.

How each one is thought to work

Lab studies suggest ashwagandha compounds, including the withanolides, may affect GABA signaling and cortisol. In people, a 2024 meta-analysis pooling nine RCTs and 558 participants found modest reductions in stress and anxiety scores in trials lasting about 6–12 weeks, with a small drop in cortisol whose confidence interval came close to no effect. The authors said long-term safety needs more data. A 2025 conference abstract (not yet peer-reviewed) pooling 15 trials reported similar results.

Rhodiola's compounds — rosavins and salidroside — are thought, mainly from lab studies, to influence brain chemicals such as serotonin, dopamine and norepinephrine and to reduce the perception of fatigue. The human evidence is weaker. A 2012 systematic review of 11 trials found contradictory results: some small trials suggested less mental fatigue, but all had a high or unclear risk of bias. A 2025 randomized crossover trial in 27 trained athletes found that a week of Rhodiola (200 mg and 1,500 mg/day — the higher dose is far above typical supplement doses) improved some Stroop cognitive-test scores versus placebo. The NIH's NCCIH says there isn't enough reliable evidence to know whether Rhodiola helps.

Is there a head-to-head trial? Not really

The closest is a 2026 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study in 186 adults with high stress. Over 60 days, both an ashwagandha extract and a Rhodiola-containing multi-herb formula beat placebo on perceived stress. But because the "Rhodiola" formula also contained ashwagandha (plus holy basil, milky oats and schisandra) and the two products were never statistically compared, this trial cannot tell us which herb is better. It was funded by the manufacturer of both products.

Which one might suit you?

Ashwagandha may be the better fit if your stress has an anxious-tense quality: trouble falling asleep, racing thoughts at night, a wired-but-tired pattern. Several small trials report lower stress scores and lower cortisol. If anxiety, panic or insomnia is significant or lasting, see a doctor — therapy and other proven treatments come first.

Rhodiola may be the better fit for burnout-pattern fatigue — depleted, low motivation, mentally foggy — or if you want something for the morning that is not sedating. In an uncontrolled study of 100 people with prolonged fatigue, people reported feeling better within a week, but without a placebo group we can't tell how much was the herb. Persistent fatigue deserves a doctor's visit: thyroid problems, anemia, sleep apnea and depression are common causes.

Both? Some people take Rhodiola in the morning and ashwagandha in the evening, but this combination hasn't been well studied. If you try both, introduce them two weeks apart so you can tell which is doing what.

Doses used in studies

  • Ashwagandha: KSM-66 at 300 mg twice daily with meals — the protocol behind a 2012 trial in 64 people using the manufacturer's extract, which reported a 27.9% cortisol drop versus 7.9% on placebo over 60 days — or Sensoril at its label dose (typically 125–250 mg daily). Give it 6–8 weeks before judging, and don't continue past about 3 months without checking with your doctor, since long-term safety is unknown. Read the safety section below before buying.
  • Rhodiola: Rhodiola rosea standardized to 3% rosavins and 1% salidroside, 200–400 mg, usually in the morning because it can disturb sleep. Check the label says Rhodiola rosea — most studies used this species, not cheaper Rhodiola crenulata.

Safety and who should skip them

Ashwagandha is generally well tolerated in short trials (GI upset, drowsiness and headache are the common complaints), but:

  • Long-term safety beyond about 3 months is unknown (NCCIH).
  • Rare cases of liver injury have been reported (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements). Stop and see a doctor if you notice yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, itching, or upper-right belly pain.
  • Avoid it if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, if you have any thyroid disorder or take thyroid medication, if you have an autoimmune disease, or if you have hormone-sensitive prostate cancer.
  • Check with a prescriber if you take diabetes, blood-pressure, immunosuppressant, sedative or seizure medicines.

Rhodiola is considered possibly safe for up to 12 weeks; safety beyond that is unknown (NCCIH). It can cause dizziness, headache, jitteriness and insomnia, especially if taken late in the day. Little is known about its safety in pregnancy or breastfeeding, so avoid it then, and avoid it if you have bipolar disorder. Talk to a prescriber if you take antidepressants (including MAOIs and SSRIs), stimulants, or blood-pressure medicines such as losartan.

The honest bottom line

Ashwagandha has the more consistent (though still modest) trial evidence for stress; Rhodiola's evidence is weaker. They are not interchangeable — wired and anxious points more toward ashwagandha, burnt out and foggy more toward Rhodiola — and both should be short-term experiments, not long-term fixes.
