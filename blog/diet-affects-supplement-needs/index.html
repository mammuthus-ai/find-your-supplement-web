How Your Diet Shapes Your Supplement Needs

Your diet is one of the strongest clues to which nutrients you may be short on, alongside age, medications, health conditions and sun exposure. Here's what each major diet gets right — and where the gaps are.

Diet is one of the strongest supplement signals

A lot of supplement advice ignores one of the most important variables: what you eat. Your dietary pattern shapes which nutrients you get regularly, which are missing from your food, and which you may absorb less well. Age, medications (such as metformin or acid-reducing drugs), health conditions, pregnancy and sun exposure matter too. A sensible supplement plan starts with an honest look at all of these — not with a generic multivitamin that ignores everything specific about you.

In our quiz, your diet is one of the main inputs we use to suggest what to look at first. Some gaps are well documented: a systematic review of 40 studies found that B12 deficiency is common in vegetarians, ranging widely by study (up to about 86% of adults in some groups), and highest in vegans who don't supplement. Here is what the evidence shows for each major pattern.

Vegan and whole-food plant-based

Vegan diets have the most clearly defined gaps in the literature — not because they are unhealthy, but because animal products are the main natural source of several important nutrients.

Vitamin B12 is non-negotiable. Natural B12 comes from animal foods; algae, mushrooms and fermented foods are not reliable sources. Fortified foods such as some plant milks, cereals and nutritional yeast are reliable sources. Deficiency develops slowly as body stores run down, but the consequences — nerve damage and megaloblastic anemia — can be serious and sometimes irreversible. Take vitamin B12 (cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin — both work), for example 1,000–2,000 mcg/day or 2,000 mcg three times weekly. Smaller daily doses (around 25–100 mcg) also work for most vegans. B12 has no established upper limit.

Omega-3 is worth considering. Your body converts plant ALA (flax, chia, walnuts) to EPA only in small amounts and to DHA even less (conversion is somewhat higher in women), so an algae-oil supplement is the simplest way for vegans to get EPA and DHA. Fish get their omega-3 from algae, too. An algae oil providing roughly 250–500 mg combined EPA+DHA per day is a reasonable target; there is no official intake requirement for EPA/DHA.

Also worth attention:

  • Vitamin D3 — vegan D3 made from lichen is the same molecule as animal-derived D3, and D3 generally raises blood vitamin D levels more, and for longer, than D2 — especially with weekly or monthly doses.
  • Iron — plant (non-heme) iron is absorbed less well, so vegetarians need about 1.8 times as much iron as meat-eaters. Pair iron-rich meals with vitamin C (citrus, bell peppers), and test ferritin before supplementing.
  • Zinc — phytic acid in legumes, grains and seeds lowers absorption, so vegetarians may need up to 50% more zinc than the RDA. If you supplement, common forms such as zinc gluconate, citrate or picolinate are all reasonable; keep total zinc at or below 40 mg/day.
  • Creatine — creatine comes mainly from meat and fish (your body also makes some). Small studies found creatine improved memory in vegetarians more than in meat-eaters.
  • Iodine — seaweed iodine content is unpredictable and many vegans use non-iodized salt; 150 mcg/day is appropriate. Avoid kelp supplements, which can deliver far more than the 1,100 mcg/day upper limit.

Ketogenic and low-carb

Keto cuts out most fruits, legumes and grains — which also cuts out important micronutrient sources. Many people feel tired, headachy or crampy in the first weeks of keto ("keto flu"). Fluid and salt losses early on likely play a role, and getting enough sodium, potassium and magnesium from food may help. If your diet is low in magnesium-rich foods, a magnesium supplement of up to 350 mg/day (the upper limit for supplemental magnesium) can help; check with a doctor first if you have kidney disease. Also watch potassium (the adequate intake is 2,600 mg/day for women and 3,400 mg/day for men; get it from foods like avocado, leafy greens and nuts — don't take potassium pills or salt substitutes without medical advice if you have kidney disease or take blood-pressure medicines), folate (leafy greens are restricted — folic acid and 5-MTHF both work, even with MTHFR variants), and vitamin C, since keto restricts the fruits that supply it. Psyllium husk or acacia fiber can help keep you regular when carbs are restricted.

Carnivore

Meat-only diets are controversial, but the gaps are predictable. Liver is a rich source of folate but contains very little vitamin C — and most modern carnivore dieters eat mostly muscle meat, which provides little of either. Historically animals were eaten nose-to-tail; modern carnivore eating needs regular organ meat or supplementation. Don't overdo liver, though — it is very high in vitamin A; about one small serving a week is plenty, and pregnant women should be especially careful. Magnesium is also present in meat at lower concentrations than in nuts, seeds and greens.

Mediterranean and omnivore

Mediterranean diets have the most favorable profile and the most extensive outcome data of any pattern. Even so, three gaps are common: vitamin D (few foods contain much vitamin D — even three servings of fatty fish a week supply less than the daily RDA — so most people rely on sun, fortified foods or a supplement), magnesium (about half of Americans get less than the average requirement from food; some studies also suggest the mineral content of certain crops has fallen modestly over decades, likely because of higher-yield crop varieties — magnesium in UK fruit and vegetables fell about 10% between 1940 and 2019, according to one analysis), and omega-3 (unless you eat oily fish such as salmon, mackerel, sardines or herring about twice a week, your EPA/DHA intake is probably low).

A short list worth discussing

Three supplements commonly worth discussing with your doctor are vitamin D, magnesium and omega-3 — but whether you need them depends on your diet, sun exposure and health. In the large VITAL trial (about 25,900 adults), 1 g/day of omega-3 and 2,000 IU/day of vitamin D3 did not reduce major heart events or cancer in the general population, so these are not must-takes for everyone.

  • Vitamin D3 — most adults get enough with 600–800 IU/day (the RDA), especially in winter or with little sun. Higher doses should follow a blood test and a clinician's advice; the Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline advises against routinely going above the RDA in healthy adults under 75.
  • Magnesium — if your diet is low in nuts, seeds, legumes and greens, a supplement of 100–350 mg/day can fill the gap (stay at or under 350 mg/day from supplements).
  • Omega-3 EPA/DHA — if you rarely eat oily fish, a fish-oil or algae-oil supplement can raise your intake.

Safety notes. Check with a doctor before taking magnesium or potassium if you have kidney disease. Omega-3 at higher doses can add to the effect of blood thinners such as warfarin, and doses above 1 g/day have been linked to a higher risk of atrial fibrillation in people with heart disease. Don't take iron unless a blood test shows you need it (it can build up dangerously in people with hemochromatosis), and keep iron supplements away from children — accidental overdose can be fatal. Keep total zinc at or below 40 mg/day. If you're pregnant, avoid large amounts of liver or vitamin A supplements.

Everything beyond the basics depends on your specific diet, lifestyle and symptoms. Blood tests for B12 and ferritin are useful if you're at risk; ask your doctor whether a vitamin D test makes sense for you. A registered dietitian or physician can tailor advice to your health history.
