Supplements for Joint Pain Over 50: What the Meta-Analyses Actually Show

Glucosamine, collagen, curcumin, omega-3, Boswellia — the joint supplement aisle is a wall of competing claims. We pulled recent meta-analyses and randomized trials to grade the evidence for knee and hip osteoarthritis after 50, the doses studied, the safety cautions, and why exercise and weight loss still come first.

Why joint pain after 50 is a different problem

Osteoarthritis (OA) becomes very common after 50. The pain is not just wear — inflammatory signals such as interleukin-1β, TNF-α, and matrix metalloproteinases add low-grade inflammation on top of mechanical degradation. So the goal after 50 is to reduce day-to-day pain enough to keep moving.

Start with what works best. The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guideline strongly recommends exercise and, if you are overweight, weight loss for knee and hip OA, plus topical NSAID gels for knee pain. These have bigger effects than any supplement here. Supplements are optional add-ons at best, and recent meta-analyses show most of their effects are small and based on low-quality trials. Here is what the evidence supports — and where the marketing outruns it.

1. Curcumin — promising but low-quality evidence

Evidence grade: low. A 2018 meta-analysis of eleven RCTs (1,009 people) found curcumin and Boswellia formulations improved pain and function versus placebo, but the authors said study quality was low overall and not adequate to make clinical recommendations. In a few small trials, curcumin performed about as well as NSAIDs with fewer stomach side effects, but the trials were too small to prove it is equivalent. A 2026 network meta-analysis found, in low-quality trials, that only modified, enhanced-absorption curcumin formulations reduced pain versus placebo. Plain turmeric powder is poorly absorbed. Combination curcumin-plus-Boswellia products have not been shown to beat either alone.

Dose: follow the label of the specific studied product — enhanced formulations such as phytosomes are dosed very differently from plain extracts, and more is not better. View curcumin phytosome on Amazon.

Liver warning: rare but serious liver injury has been reported with high-dose and "enhanced-absorption" turmeric products. Stop and see a doctor if you get dark urine, yellow skin or eyes, or upper-right abdominal pain. Avoid curcumin if you have liver or gallbladder disease, and check with your doctor if you take blood thinners.

2. Omega-3 (EPA + DHA) — small benefit at best

Evidence grade: weak/mixed. A 2023 meta-analysis of nine RCTs (2,070 OA patients) found a small pain reduction (SMD −0.29) and better joint function versus placebo. But in people 65 and older, pooled trials showed no significant pain benefit, and the largest trial, VITAL (1,398 older adults), found none. People who already eat fatty fish regularly may gain little (this hasn't been tested). The ACR guideline conditionally recommends against fish oil for OA. Read labels — many 1,000 mg capsules contain only 300 mg of EPA+DHA.

Dose: if you try fish oil, about 0.5–1 g EPA+DHA a day was as effective as a much higher dose in the one head-to-head trial. Doses above 1 g/day raise the risk of atrial fibrillation. Choose a third-party-tested product and take it with a meal. View 500 mg EPA+DHA fish oil on Amazon.

3. Collagen peptides — moderate, but small trials

Evidence grade: moderate. A 2023 meta-analysis of four RCTs found a moderate pain reduction (standardized mean difference −0.58 versus placebo), but all four trials were at high risk of bias. An updated 2024 systematic review of 11 RCTs found improvements in knee OA pain and function, though results varied a lot between trials. Animal studies suggest the peptides may reach cartilage and stimulate collagen production; this hasn't been shown in people.

Dose: 10 g hydrolyzed collagen peptides daily. Undenatured UC-II at 40 mg is a single-capsule alternative with thinner evidence (one manufacturer-sponsored trial). If you've had kidney stones, ask your doctor first — collagen raises urinary oxalate. View hydrolyzed collagen peptides on Amazon.

4. Glucosamine + chondroitin — the old standard, still contested

Evidence grade: low, contested. The ACR guideline strongly recommends against glucosamine for knee and hip OA, noting that industry-funded trials look better than publicly funded ones, and it does not recommend chondroitin for those joints either. A 2023 meta-analysis found the combination improved total WOMAC score but not pain on the VAS scale, and a 2024 network meta-analysis ranked glucosamine plus omega-3 as the most effective combination for pain — in a network analysis with very wide uncertainty. Any average effect is small. Most positive trials used glucosamine sulfate, and these are slow-acting: give it 8–12 weeks.

Dose: glucosamine sulfate 1,500 mg + chondroitin sulfate 1,200 mg, split. If pain has not measurably improved after three months, stop. Glucosamine can raise INR in people on warfarin — avoid it or check with your doctor. Most glucosamine is made from shellfish. View glucosamine + chondroitin on Amazon.

5. Vitamin D — not effective for OA pain

Evidence grade: not effective for joint pain. Trials, including people with low vitamin D, have not shown that vitamin D supplements reduce knee OA pain: a 2-year JAMA trial in 413 people with low vitamin D and the VITAL trial both found no benefit, and the ACR guideline conditionally recommends against it for OA. Low vitamin D is common in older adults, so correct a deficiency for bone health, not for joint pain.

Dose: if a test shows you're low, follow your clinician's advice; doses above 4,000 IU/day (the upper limit) should only be taken under a doctor's supervision. See our vitamin D3 deficiency guide.

What to skip or be skeptical of

  • MSM: small, short trials with an inconsistent meta-analytic picture. Not a priority spend.
  • Oral hyaluronic acid: the evidence is limited and inconsistent.
  • SAM-e: thin, dated evidence and relatively expensive — a second-line option at best. Don't combine it with antidepressants without your doctor's advice.
  • Proprietary joint blends: a label listing one combined blend total instead of itemized doses may be underdosing the actives. Single-ingredient products make it easier to tell what is working.

The practical plan and bottom line

Start with the treatments that have the strongest evidence: regular exercise (strength training and low-impact aerobic activity) and, if you are overweight, weight loss — every kilogram lost reduces knee load by about 4 kg per step. If you want to try a supplement, add one at a time and judge it after 8–12 weeks, since joint biology acts slowly:

  • Collagen peptides 10 g/day have the most consistent (though small and often industry-funded) trial support.
  • Curcumin is an option if you have no liver or gallbladder disease and don't take blood thinners — use the studied product's label dose.
  • Fish oil at 0.5–1 g EPA+DHA is unlikely to help much, especially over 65.
  • Glucosamine plus chondroitin for a three-month trial at most, and not if you take warfarin.
  • Vitamin D only to correct a deficiency, for bone health.

Safety summary

  • Curcumin/turmeric: rare liver injury with high-dose and enhanced-absorption products; avoid with liver or gallbladder disease.
  • Fish oil: doses above 1 g/day raise atrial fibrillation risk; avoid with a fish allergy.
  • Glucosamine: can raise INR with warfarin; usually shellfish-derived.
  • Collagen: raises urinary oxalate — ask first if you've had kidney stones.
  • Blood thinners and surgery: omega-3 and curcumin may have mild antiplatelet effects — tell your doctor before surgery or if you take anticoagulants.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding, or have kidney or liver disease or take prescription medicines? Check with your doctor before starting any of these.
