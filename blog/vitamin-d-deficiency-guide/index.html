Vitamin D Deficiency: Signs, Testing, and How to Fix It

Most people get enough vitamin D, but some groups run low without knowing it. Learn the real signs, who is at risk, when testing makes sense, and how much to take safely.

A quiet deficiency

Vitamin D deficiency can be easy to miss: it develops slowly and the early symptoms are vague.

One widely cited 2007 review estimated that about 1 billion people worldwide have low vitamin D, depending on the cutoff used. In the US, most people have adequate vitamin D, but a meaningful minority run low: NIH data show up to about 8% of some age groups are at risk of deficiency (below 12 ng/mL), and more are in the "possibly inadequate" 12–20 ng/mL range.

Vitamin D matters for more than bones. Its active form works like a hormone that switches many genes on and off and helps regulate the immune system — though trials of supplements for heart disease, cancer and depression in people who aren't deficient have mostly been disappointing.

Who is most at risk?

  • Geographic location — the farther you live from the equator, the longer your "vitamin D winter": in Boston (42°N) winter sun makes essentially no vitamin D from November to February, and in Edmonton (52°N) from October to March (Webb 1988).
  • Indoor lifestyle — office workers, students and homebound people often go weeks without meaningful sun exposure. Even people in sunny climates can run low if they work indoors.
  • Darker skin tones — melanin acts as a natural sunscreen. People with darker skin make less vitamin D from the same sun exposure, and tend to have lower blood levels.
  • Age — the skin's ability to make vitamin D falls substantially with age — by more than half in older adults.
  • Obesity — vitamin D is fat-soluble and gets stored in fat tissue, so people with obesity may need higher intakes to reach the same blood levels.
  • Malabsorption and diet — celiac disease, Crohn's disease and bariatric surgery all impair absorption. Vitamin D occurs naturally in very few foods — mainly fatty fish, egg yolks and organ meats — so vegans rely on fortified foods, UV-treated mushrooms, sun or a supplement.

Signs and symptoms

Deficiency rarely shows up dramatically — the symptoms are vague, overlapping and easy to blame on stress or aging.

  • Bone pain or achiness — particularly in the back, hips and legs
  • Muscle weakness — difficulty climbing stairs or rising from chairs
  • Tiredness — common but nonspecific
  • Frequent respiratory infections — low vitamin D is linked to more infections, and supplements may modestly reduce them in some groups
  • Low mood — low vitamin D is linked with depression in studies, but supplements haven't been shown to prevent or treat it

Some studies also link low vitamin D to poorer thinking, but supplements haven't been shown to improve it.

The challenge: all of these are nonspecific. You cannot reliably self-diagnose vitamin D deficiency from symptoms alone — only a blood test can confirm it.

How to test your vitamin D

The correct test is serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D), sometimes written as 25-OH vitamin D or calcidiol. Routine testing isn't recommended for healthy adults (Endocrine Society 2024). Ask your doctor about a 25(OH)D test if you have symptoms of deficiency, bone disease, malabsorption, have had bariatric surgery, or take medicines that affect vitamin D. Direct-to-consumer labs also offer it (prices vary).

Here is how the US National Academies interpret results:

Status25(OH)D result
DeficientBelow 12 ng/mL (30 nmol/L)
Possibly inadequate12–19 ng/mL (30–49 nmol/L)
Adequate for most people20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L) or higher
No added benefit, potential for harmAbove 50 ng/mL (125 nmol/L)
ToxicityUsually above 150 ng/mL (375 nmol/L)

How to supplement effectively

For adults who are low, 1,000–2,000 IU/day of vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is a common maintenance dose. Don't exceed 4,000 IU/day — the upper limit set by the US National Academies — unless a doctor prescribes it. A plain vitamin D3 1,000 IU supplement covers most needs.

  • D3 over D2 — D3 generally raises and maintains blood levels better than D2. Vegan D3 from lichen is the same molecule as animal-derived D3.
  • Take it with food — vitamin D is fat-soluble. Take it with a meal that contains some fat: one 2015 study in 50 older adults found about 32% higher absorption with a fat-containing meal than a fat-free one.
  • About vitamin K2 — some people pair D3 with vitamin K2; the idea is that K2 helps direct calcium to bone, though this hasn't been proven in outcome trials. If you take warfarin or another vitamin K antagonist, don't take K2 without talking to your doctor.
  • Magnesium — magnesium is involved in activating vitamin D, so make sure your diet supplies enough (nuts, seeds, legumes, leafy greens).

If you are confirmed deficient, your doctor may prescribe a short course of higher doses, then retest. Large single "bolus" doses are generally avoided — one trial found an annual 500,000 IU dose increased falls and fractures in older women.

Safety notes. Talk to your doctor first if you take thiazide diuretics or steroids, or have kidney disease, sarcoidosis or high calcium. Vitamin D needs during pregnancy are the same as for other adults (600 IU/day); ask your prenatal care provider before taking more.

The bottom line

Low vitamin D is common in some groups — people who get little sun, have darker skin, are older, have obesity or have absorption problems — and it is easy to correct. If you have a medical risk factor, ask your doctor whether testing is right for you. Without a test, 600–800 IU/day (the RDA) — or up to 1,000–2,000 IU/day in winter or with little sun — is a safe choice for most adults. Stay at or under 4,000 IU/day.
