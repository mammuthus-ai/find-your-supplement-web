Omega-3 for Vegans and Vegetarians: The Complete Algae Oil Guide

Fish don't actually make omega-3 — they get it from algae. Here's why algae oil is a practical choice for plant-based eaters.

By the Find Your Supplement team · Published February 10, 2026 · Updated October 9, 2026

The Omega-3 Problem on Plant-Based Diets

Vegans and vegetarians typically have low blood levels of EPA and DHA, similar to omnivores who rarely eat fish (Sarter et al., 2015). The long-chain EPA and DHA are found almost exclusively in marine sources such as fish, seafood and algae.

ALA (alpha-linolenic acid), the short-chain omega-3 found in flaxseed, walnuts, and chia seeds, converts to EPA and DHA in the body. But only a small fraction of ALA becomes EPA (estimates are roughly 5–10%), and very little, often under 1%, becomes DHA, especially in men (Burdge et al., 2002).

Where Fish Really Get Their Omega-3

Here's the key insight: fish don't synthesize EPA and DHA. They get them by eating microalgae and other marine organisms. The fatty acids bioaccumulate up the food chain.

This means you can go directly to the source — algae — and skip the fish entirely. Algae oil provides DHA directly, and some products also contain EPA, so check the label (NCCIH). It also avoids concerns about mercury, PCBs, overfishing, and fish smell. In one study, algal DHA capsules raised blood DHA about as well as cooked salmon (Arterburn et al., 2008), and in vegans a daily algae supplement of about 250 mg raised omega-3 blood levels (Sarter et al., 2015).

EPA vs DHA: What's the Difference?

EPA (Eicosapentaenoic Acid):

  • Involved in regulating inflammation
  • EPA-rich formulas have shown modest benefit for depressive symptoms in some trials
  • Studied for heart health, mostly in fish-oil and prescription EPA trials

DHA (Docosahexaenoic Acid):

  • DHA is the main omega-3 fat in the brain and retina
  • Important for infant brain and eye development
  • Eye health (concentrated in the retina)

Studies have used a wide range of doses, and there is no official separate target for EPA or DHA. Health bodies suggest about 250–500 mg/day of EPA+DHA for general health (EFSA: 250 mg/day). Higher amounts are sometimes used for specific conditions under medical advice.

Choosing an Algae Oil Supplement

When evaluating algae oil supplements, look for:

  • Total EPA+DHA content per serving — look for roughly 250–500 mg EPA+DHA per daily serving
  • EPA as well as DHA: many algae oils are DHA-only, so check the label if you want both
  • Absorption: take it with a meal containing fat for best absorption
  • Third-party testing: Look for IFOS or similar certification
  • Freshness: Omega-3s oxidize easily. Check the oxidation markers (TOTOX score)
  • No filler oils: Some supplements dilute with cheap vegetable oils

Fish Oil vs Algae Oil

Both provide bioavailable EPA and DHA (check algae oil labels for EPA). The choice often comes down to:

FactorFish OilAlgae Oil
EPA+DHA contentHighModerate–High
SustainabilityVariableMore sustainable
Mercury/PCBsVery low in purified productsNone
Suitable for vegansNoYes
Fish odor/burpsCommonMinimal

For vegans and vegetarians, algae oil is the practical choice. For omnivores, high-quality fish oil works too.

Safety

Omega-3 supplements are generally well tolerated, but trials have linked them to a higher risk of atrial fibrillation, especially above 1 g/day (Gencer et al., 2021). If you have heart rhythm problems or take blood thinners, check with your clinician first.

Evidence Level: Moderate

Algae oil reliably raises blood DHA. Heart benefits from omega-3 supplements are small and come mainly from fish-oil trials. A 2019 meta-analysis of 13 trials of marine omega-3 supplements (Hu et al., 2019) found small reductions (about 3–8%) in heart attack and cardiovascular death. The large VITAL trial (Manson et al., 2019) found no significant effect on major cardiovascular events. These trials used fish-derived oils, not algae oil.
