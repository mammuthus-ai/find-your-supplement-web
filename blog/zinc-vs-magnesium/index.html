Zinc vs Magnesium: Which Mineral Do You Actually Need?

Zinc and magnesium do completely different jobs in the body. This research-backed comparison breaks down what each one does, who tends to run low, safe doses and upper limits, and who might consider zinc, magnesium (or both).

Two minerals, two different jobs

Many people eat less magnesium than recommended, and some groups (vegetarians, older adults, people with gut conditions) run low on zinc. True deficiency of either is uncommon in healthy people (NIH ODS zinc, NIH ODS magnesium). Both are involved in hundreds of biochemical processes — but they are not interchangeable. Here is what each mineral actually does, who tends to run low, and how to choose.

What zinc actually does

Zinc is an essential trace mineral — needed only in small amounts, but required for the activity of hundreds of enzymes. What the evidence shows:

  • Immune function and colds (moderate — shortens colds if started early). Zinc is critical for the development and function of immune cells; even mild deficiency measurably reduces natural killer cell activity (Prasad, 2008). Zinc acetate or gluconate lozenges started within 24 hours of the first symptoms shortened colds by about a third in trials (Hemilä, 2017). Those trials used about 80–90 mg zinc a day — over the normal 40 mg upper limit — so use lozenges only for the few days of a cold, not long-term, check with your doctor or pharmacist first, and never use zinc nasal sprays (linked to loss of smell).
  • Testosterone (limited — mainly if you're low in zinc). A 2023 review of mostly animal and a few human studies found zinc and testosterone are linked, with supplements mainly helping men who are low in zinc. In a small uncontrolled study of 9 zinc-deficient older men, testosterone roughly doubled after 6 months of zinc. It restores normal rather than enhancing above it.
  • Wound healing and skin. Zinc is needed for wound healing; supplements help mainly if you're deficient (NIH ODS).
  • Taste and smell (for deficiency). Zinc is involved in taste; low zinc can dull taste and smell — a legitimate reason to ask your doctor about checking your zinc.
  • Blood sugar (limited). A meta-analysis of 32 studies in 1,700 people found zinc lowered fasting glucose, insulin, and hemoglobin A1c, mostly in diabetes and prediabetes — though NIH says the evidence isn't strong enough to recommend zinc for diabetes.

Most likely to run low: vegetarians and vegans (phytates block absorption), people with GI disorders or after bariatric surgery, heavy alcohol users, pregnant and breastfeeding women, and older adults. Possible signs include frequent infections, slow wound healing, hair loss, or lost taste and smell — though all of these have many other causes.

What magnesium actually does

Magnesium is a macromineral — you need far more of it than zinc — and a cofactor in more than 300 enzyme systems spanning DNA synthesis, muscle contraction, nerve transmission, and energy production.

  • Sleep (limited). Proposed mechanisms include calming effects on the nervous system; a 2024 systematic review of small, mixed studies found it may benefit people with mild insomnia and anxiety, particularly with low magnesium status, and called for larger trials. If insomnia is persistent, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the recommended first-line treatment.
  • Anxiety and mood (limited). A 2017 review of 18 studies suggested a benefit in anxiety-prone people, but rated the evidence as poor quality.
  • Migraine prevention (moderate). The American Academy of Neurology and American Headache Society rate magnesium as "probably effective", though data are limited (NIH ODS).
  • Muscle cramps (not supported). A Cochrane review found magnesium unlikely to prevent typical night-time leg cramps in older adults.
  • Blood sugar (limited). A 2020 trial (60 patients, 250 mg magnesium oxide + 150 mg zinc sulfate) found 12 weeks of the combination lowered fasting glucose and insulin levels in people with type 2 diabetes and heart disease.

Most likely to run low: many Americans eat less magnesium than recommended (NIH). Highest risk: type 2 diabetes, GI conditions, long-term PPI or diuretic use, regular heavy alcohol use, and older adults.

Can you take both together?

Yes — the combination is generally safe at standard doses. Very high-dose zinc can interfere with magnesium absorption, seen around 142 mg of zinc per day (Spencer et al., 1994) — far above any normal dose. Practical approach: zinc with a meal, magnesium in the evening.

Which form and dose

Zinc: gluconate, citrate, picolinate and glycinate are all reasonable; avoid zinc oxide. Take 8–15 mg for maintenance, always with food. Confirm deficiency with your clinician before using 25–40 mg short-term, and pair anything above 25 mg/day long-term with 1–2 mg copper. Try zinc picolinate 15 mg. Zinc can interact with some antibiotics, so space them apart.

Magnesium: citrate, glycinate or chloride are good choices; magnesium oxide is less well absorbed than citrate or chloride. Take 200–350 mg in the evening, starting at 200 mg to avoid loose stools. Higher doses used for migraine (400–600 mg) should only be taken with your doctor's OK — and don't take magnesium supplements if you have kidney disease without medical advice. Separate magnesium from some antibiotics and osteoporosis drugs by a few hours. Try magnesium glycinate 200 mg.

The bottom line

Consider zinc if you eat a plant-based diet, have a gut condition, or have lost taste or smell, and use short-term lozenges at the start of a cold. If you have symptoms of low testosterone, see a doctor; zinc helps only if you're low in zinc. Consider magnesium if your diet is low in it, you get migraines (with your doctor), or you want a low-risk option for mild sleep or stress problems. Consider testing or a dietitian review if you fit several risk groups. Discuss supplements with your healthcare provider — the upper limits exist for good reason. Unsure where to start?
