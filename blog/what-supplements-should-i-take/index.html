What Supplements Should I Take? A Personalized, Evidence-Based Guide

With thousands of supplements on the market, deciding what to take can be overwhelming. Here's a systematic, evidence-based approach to figuring out exactly what your body needs.

The Supplement Paradox

Walk into any health food store or open Amazon, and you will find thousands of supplements promising to fix everything from fatigue to aging. The supplement industry is a $177 billion market — and most of it is built on confusion.

Here is the honest truth: most people are taking supplements they do not need, and missing the ones they actually do. A blanket multivitamin does not solve a magnesium deficiency caused by chronic stress. High-dose vitamin C will not help the fatigue caused by undiagnosed B12 deficiency in a vegan.

Personalization is everything. The supplements that are right for you depend on four intersecting factors: your health goals, your diet, your lifestyle, and your symptoms.

Factor 1: Your Health Goals

Goals give a starting direction — but they are the weakest signal of the four. Everyone wants more energy and better sleep. Used to filter your shortlist, though, they matter:

  • Energy: B vitamins (especially B12), iron, CoQ10, and vitamin D
  • Sleep: Magnesium glycinate, melatonin, and ashwagandha all have RCT evidence
  • Muscle and recovery: Creatine monohydrate has the most evidence of any sports supplement
  • Immunity: Vitamin D3, zinc, and vitamin C have the strongest immune support data
  • Focus and cognition: Omega-3 (DHA specifically), creatine, and B12 top the evidence list
  • Longevity: Omega-3, NAC, CoQ10, and magnesium have the strongest aging-related data

Factor 2: Your Diet — The Strongest Signal

Your diet is the single most powerful predictor of which supplements you need, because dietary patterns create predictable, well-documented deficiencies.

Vegan and vegetarian diets have the most clearly defined gaps. Vitamin B12 is essentially zero in plant foods — studies show 93–95% of vegans are deficient without supplementation, making it non-negotiable. ALA from flaxseed converts to EPA at only 5–10% efficiency, so algae oil is the direct route to EPA and DHA. Non-heme plant iron is far less bioavailable than heme iron, plant phytates inhibit zinc absorption (vegetarians may need 50% more), and creatine exists only in animal tissue.

Keto and carnivore diets create different gaps: vitamin C, magnesium and potassium, and folate from leafy greens.

Standard Western diets typically fall short on magnesium (soil depletion plus processed food), omega-3 (a 15:1 omega-6 to omega-3 ratio versus the optimal 4:1), and vitamin D.

Factor 3: Your Lifestyle

Several lifestyle factors reliably drive up demand for specific nutrients:

  • Sun exposure: Working indoors, living above 35° latitude, or daily high-SPF sunscreen severely limits vitamin D synthesis — indoor office workers average well below the 40–60 ng/mL optimal range
  • Exercise: Intense cardio increases iron loss; weight training raises demand for creatine and magnesium
  • Stress: Chronic stress depletes magnesium rapidly — a vicious cycle. Ashwagandha (KSM-66) has RCT evidence for cortisol reduction
  • Alcohol: Even moderate drinking increases urinary loss of magnesium, zinc, folate, and B12
  • Caffeine: Heavy coffee drinkers excrete more calcium, magnesium, and B vitamins — prioritize magnesium

Factor 4: Your Symptoms — Reading the Signals

Symptoms are valuable clues, not diagnoses — many deficiencies overlap. Still, certain patterns are worth noting.

The Tier-1 Shortlist: Where to Start

If you are overwhelmed, these consistently show up in population data as the most commonly deficient and most impactful:

  • Vitamin D3 + K2 — deficient in ~70% of adults in the developed world; take with a fat-containing meal
  • Magnesium glycinate — deficient in ~68% of Americans; essential for sleep, stress, and muscle function. View on Amazon
  • Omega-3 (fish or algae oil) — unless you eat fatty fish 3x/week, you are likely low in EPA and DHA. View on Amazon
  • Vitamin B12 — essential for vegans and vegetarians; worth checking for anyone over 50

Build Your Protocol — and What to Avoid

The systematic approach: start with your diet's documented gaps, layer in lifestyle factors, map symptoms to likely deficiencies, cross-reference your goals, and prioritize the overlap — supplements that appear in multiple categories (magnesium for sleep AND stress AND exercise) come first.

While you shop, watch for red flags: proprietary blends with undisclosed doses, mega-dose formulas (fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K can accumulate to toxic levels), multivitamins with nutrients at 1–10% of effective doses just to pad the label, and heavily marketed botanicals — echinacea, green tea extract, resveratrol — with weak clinical evidence.

Bottom Line

The right supplements for you are not the same as for your partner, your friend, or the influencer you follow. Your diet, lifestyle, and physiology create a unique nutrient fingerprint.
