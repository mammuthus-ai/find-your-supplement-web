What Supplements Should I Take? A Personalized, Evidence-Based Guide

With thousands of supplements on the market, deciding what to take can be overwhelming. Here's a systematic, evidence-based approach to figuring out what you may actually need.

The Supplement Paradox

Walk into any health food store or open Amazon, and you will find thousands of supplements promising to fix everything from fatigue to aging. The supplement industry is a market worth well over $100 billion a year worldwide — and a lot of its marketing thrives on confusion.

Here is the honest truth: many people take supplements without knowing whether they need them. A multivitamin may not contain enough of a nutrient you're actually low in, such as magnesium. High-dose vitamin C will not help the fatigue caused by undiagnosed B12 deficiency in a vegan.

Personalization matters. The supplements that make sense for you depend on four intersecting factors: your health goals, your diet, your lifestyle, and your symptoms.

Factor 1: Your Health Goals

Goals give a starting direction — but they are the weakest signal of the four. Everyone wants more energy and better sleep. Used to filter your shortlist, though, they matter:

  • Energy: B vitamins (especially B12), iron and vitamin D help mainly if you're low in them. CoQ10 is popular, but evidence for everyday tiredness is limited.
  • Sleep: magnesium, melatonin and ashwagandha have small RCTs showing modest effects. For chronic insomnia, CBT-I (cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia) is the first-line treatment (AASM, 2017).
  • Muscle and recovery: Creatine monohydrate has the most evidence of any sports supplement (ISSN, 2017)
  • Immunity: correcting low vitamin D or zinc may help. Benefits for people who aren't deficient are small or unproven (Endocrine Society, 2024).
  • Focus and cognition: B12 matters if you're low. Evidence for omega-3 and creatine in healthy adults is limited.

Factor 2: Your Diet — The Strongest Signal

Your diet is the single most powerful predictor of which supplements you need, because dietary patterns create predictable, well-documented gaps.

Vegan and vegetarian diets have the most clearly defined gaps. Vitamin B12 is essentially absent from unfortified plant foods, and studies consistently find high rates of low B12 in vegans who don't supplement (Pawlak et al., 2013; Niklewicz et al., 2024). A B12 supplement or fortified foods are essential. ALA from flaxseed converts to EPA at only 5–10% efficiency, so algae oil is the direct route to EPA and DHA. Non-heme plant iron is less bioavailable than heme iron, plant phytates inhibit zinc absorption (vegetarians may need 50% more), and creatine exists only in animal tissue.

Very low-carb diets (keto, carnivore) can be low in vitamin C, magnesium, potassium and folate if they exclude vegetables.

Standard Western diets: many people eat less magnesium than recommended, little omega-3 from fish, and little vitamin D from food.

Factor 3: Your Lifestyle

Several lifestyle factors can affect specific nutrients:

  • Sun exposure: Little sun exposure, especially in winter at northern latitudes, lowers vitamin D production. Most people can meet needs with the recommended daily intake. NHS suggests 10 micrograms (400 IU) a day in autumn and winter (NHS).
  • Exercise: Intense endurance training can increase iron losses. Creatine has strong evidence for strength-training performance.
  • Stress: Some research suggests stress and low magnesium may be linked, but this isn't settled. Standardized ashwagandha extracts have small RCTs showing lower cortisol (Lopresti et al., 2019); rare liver injury has been reported, and it isn't for pregnancy or thyroid and autoimmune conditions (NCCIH).
  • Alcohol: Heavy drinking can deplete magnesium, zinc and folate.
  • Caffeine: Caffeine slightly increases calcium and magnesium in urine, which matters little if your diet is adequate.

The Tier-1 Shortlist: Where to Start

If you are overwhelmed, these are common gaps worth checking first — ideally by looking at your diet or with a blood test:

  • Vitamin D3 — nearly 1 in 4 Americans have low or inadequate levels (Herrick et al., 2019), and more in winter and in people with darker skin. Take it with a meal containing fat, and stay under 4,000 IU (100 micrograms) a day unless a doctor advises otherwise. If you choose a D3 + K2 combination, do not take vitamin K (K1 or K2) if you take warfarin or another vitamin K antagonist (NHS).
  • Magnesium — about two-thirds of Americans eat less than the recommended amount (King et al., 2005). It's needed for nerve and muscle function; evidence that supplements improve sleep is limited. From supplements, stay at or under 350 mg/day of elemental magnesium (the US upper limit for supplemental magnesium) unless a doctor advises more, and avoid it if you have kidney disease unless your doctor approves. Build Your Protocol — and What to Avoid

The systematic approach: start with your diet's documented gaps, layer in lifestyle factors, map symptoms to likely deficiencies, cross-reference your goals, and prioritize the overlap — supplements that appear in multiple categories (for example, B12 for a tired vegan over 50) come first.

While you shop, watch for red flags: proprietary blends with undisclosed doses, mega-dose formulas (fat-soluble vitamins, especially A and D, can accumulate to toxic levels), multivitamins with nutrients at 1–10% of effective doses just to pad the label, and heavily marketed botanicals — echinacea, green tea extract, resveratrol — with weak clinical evidence (and concentrated green tea extract has been linked to liver injury).

Bottom Line

The right supplements for you are not the same as for your partner, your friend, or the influencer you follow. Your diet, lifestyle, and health create a unique picture. Our free quiz walks through these same four factors and suggests options to discuss with your clinician, in about two minutes.

Always consult a healthcare provider before starting any new supplement regimen, particularly if you take medications (for example, warfarin), have kidney disease or other health conditions, or are pregnant.
