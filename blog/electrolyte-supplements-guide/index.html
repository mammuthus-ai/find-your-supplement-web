Do You Need Electrolyte Supplements? When Water Is Enough and When It Isn't

Do you need electrolyte supplements, or is water enough? For most people on most days, food and water cover it, but long sweaty workouts and bouts of diarrhea or vomiting are different. Here's what the research shows, and who should check with a doctor first.

By the Find Your Supplement team · Published November 13, 2026

Why electrolyte packets are everywhere

Electrolyte powders have moved from the marathon start line to the office desk. Electrolytes are minerals that carry an electric charge in body fluids, including sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium and magnesium. They help balance fluids and keep nerves and muscles working. You lose some in sweat, and diarrhea or vomiting can drain them quickly.

Most of us already get plenty of sodium

Sodium is the main electrolyte lost in sweat, and most of us overdo it. Americans eat more than 3,300 mg a day on average, well above the federal advice of less than 2,300 mg for teens and adults. Too much can raise blood pressure and the risk of heart disease and stroke (CDC). Some electrolyte mixes add 500 to 1,000 mg per serving, so check the label.

Typical sports drinks also don't hydrate you better than water on an ordinary day. In a 2016 UK trial, 72 well-hydrated, fasted men drank 1 liter each of water and three other drinks on separate occasions. Over 4 hours they peed out less after an oral rehydration solution or milk than after water, but a sports drink was no different. The study only measured short-term fluid retention at rest (Maughan et al., 2016).

When electrolytes do make sense

Long, sweaty exercise

The American College of Sports Medicine advises not losing more than about 2% of your body weight through sweat during exercise, because bigger losses can hurt performance, especially in the heat. Sweat rates and sweat electrolytes vary a lot between people, so it recommends a personal plan; weighing yourself before and after shows roughly how much you sweat. Drinks with electrolytes and carbohydrates can offer benefits over water alone in certain situations, and if you're not in a hurry to recover, normal meals and drinks afterward can replace what you lost (ACSM, 2007).

Sweat sodium losses depend on things like exercise intensity, heat, how used to the heat you are, and diet (Baker, 2017). A long summer run is a very different case from a 30-minute gym session.

Diarrhea or vomiting

If you're losing a lot of fluid this way, you need to replace the sugar, salts and minerals lost, and an oral rehydration solution is made for this. If you've been sick, start with small sips (NHS).

Talk to a doctor right away for frequent vomiting, diarrhea lasting more than 2 days (more than a day in a child), a high fever, severe belly pain, black or bloody stools, or signs of dehydration such as extreme thirst, peeing less or dizziness. In young children, also watch for no wet diaper in 3 hours, no tears or drowsiness, and get help right away for a baby under 12 months with diarrhea (NIDDK). Call 911 for confusion, trouble breathing, cold or blotchy skin, or someone who is hard to wake (NHS).

Another risk at long races: drinking too much

At the 2002 Boston Marathon, 13% of 488 non-elite runners tested at the finish had low blood sodium (hyponatremia), and 0.6% had critically low levels. It was linked to gaining weight during the race, finishing in over 4 hours, and a very low or very high BMI, but not to the type of drink (Almond et al., 2005).

An expert consensus says the main cause is drinking more than you lose, whether water or sports drinks, and advises drinking to thirst. Sodium tablets won't prevent it if you overdrink. Finishing at the same weight or heavier is a sign you drank too much, though low blood sodium can happen even if you lost weight. Get urgent help for confusion, a worsening headache, vomiting or a seizure during a long event or in the 24 hours after; don't treat these by drinking more (Hew-Butler et al., 2015).

Who should check with a doctor first

Potassium. With chronic kidney disease, or on ACE inhibitors, ARBs or potassium-sparing diuretics, even normal amounts can push blood potassium too high. It can also happen with type 1 diabetes, heart failure, adrenal insufficiency or liver disease (NIH ODS). Electrolyte mixes and rehydration solutions usually contain potassium, so call your doctor or care team promptly before using any, even for a stomach bug, and don't change your medicines on your own.

Sodium and fluid. With high blood pressure, heart failure or kidney disease, ask before using salty mixes. With heart failure, your care team may also limit how much you drink (AHA).

Magnesium. The upper limit from supplements is 350 mg a day for adults; high doses can cause diarrhea, nausea and stomach cramps (NIH ODS).

For a stomach bug, look for a product labeled oral rehydration solution and ask a pharmacist which suits you or your child. For long, hot workouts, an electrolyte drink mix can be part of your plan; compare the sodium per serving.

The bottom line

Most people don't need electrolyte supplements for everyday hydration: water and meals cover it, and most of us already eat too much sodium. They make sense for diarrhea or vomiting (an oral rehydration solution) and may help in some long, sweaty workouts. At long events, drinking too much, even of sports drinks, can dangerously lower blood sodium, so drink to thirst. With kidney or heart disease, or medicines that affect potassium, ask your doctor first.
