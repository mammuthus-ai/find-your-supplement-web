The Science Behind Ashwagandha: Does It Actually Work?

Standardized ashwagandha root extracts have small clinical trials suggesting benefits for stress and cortisol, with weaker evidence for testosterone. We review the evidence.

By the Find Your Supplement team · Published February 24, 2026 · Updated October 9, 2026

From Ayurveda to Clinical Trials

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) has been used in Ayurvedic medicine for centuries as a rasayana — a herb for longevity and vitality. In the past two decades, it has been tested in a number of small, short double-blind, placebo-controlled trials. The results are promising but not definitive.

What the Evidence Shows

Stress and cortisol. A 2019 RCT published in Medicine (Lopresti et al., 2019) gave 240 mg of a standardized ashwagandha extract (Shoden, a different extract from KSM-66) or placebo to 60 stressed but healthy adults for 60 days. The ashwagandha group had:

  • 23% lower cortisol levels (placebo: essentially no change)
  • Lower anxiety on one scale (HAM-A), although the improvement on a second questionnaire (DASS-21) was not statistically significant
  • Lower DHEA-S, another stress-linked hormone

Several other small RCTs point in the same direction, though larger independent trials are still needed. Most trials so far have been short (8–12 weeks), and many were run with extract manufacturers. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) still describes the evidence on anxiety as unclear (NCCIH).

Testosterone and male fertility. A 2010 uncontrolled study in Fertility and Sterility (Ahmad et al., 2010) found that infertile men taking ashwagandha root had better sperm count, motility and testosterone afterwards. It had no placebo group. A 2019 crossover RCT (Lopresti et al., 2019) in overweight men aged 40–70 found testosterone rose about 15% more than with placebo, although fatigue, vigor and sexual well-being did not improve. Most testosterone data come from small trials in infertile, older or overweight men.

Sleep quality. Small RCTs (2019–2021) found modest improvements in time to fall asleep and sleep efficiency, mainly in people with insomnia taking about 600 mg/day for 8+ weeks. In the 2019 trial of 60 people with insomnia, those taking ashwagandha fell asleep in about 29 minutes on average versus about 34 minutes on placebo (Langade et al., 2019; see also Langade et al., 2021). A 2021 meta-analysis of 5 trials called the overall effect small but significant (Cheah et al., 2021). A mouse study suggests a compound in ashwagandha leaves (triethylene glycol) may promote sleep (Kaushik et al., 2017). This hasn't been shown in people or in root-only extracts. For chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the recommended first treatment.

Strength and muscle recovery. A 2015 RCT in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition (Wankhede et al., 2015) found that 57 young men new to resistance training who took KSM-66 (300 mg twice daily) during an 8-week program had greater strength gains than placebo (bench press up about 46 kg vs 26 kg) and lower levels of a muscle-damage marker. It was one small study.

The Right Form Matters

KSM-66 (root only), Sensoril (root and leaf) and Shoden are the most studied standardized extracts. Generic ashwagandha powder without standardization may have highly variable potency.

No trials have compared extracts head-to-head, so pick one that matches the dose and form used in studies for your goal:

  • KSM-66: used in the sleep and strength trials above (300 mg twice daily).
  • Shoden: used in the cortisol and testosterone trials above (240 mg/day).
  • Sensoril: a root-and-leaf extract with its own, smaller set of trials.

Dosing and How Long to Take It

Typical dose in trials: 300–600 mg/day of KSM-66 extract (often 300 mg twice daily), or 240 mg/day of Shoden.

Studies have mostly lasted up to 3 months, and safety beyond that is unknown. Talk to a clinician before using it longer.

Who Should Avoid It?

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women
  • People with autoimmune disease, including autoimmune thyroid disease (Hashimoto's, Graves') — ashwagandha can increase thyroid hormone levels
  • People with hormone-sensitive prostate cancer
  • Those on thyroid medications — dose adjustments may be needed
  • People taking immunosuppressants

Rare cases of liver injury have been reported; stop and see a doctor if you notice yellow skin or eyes, dark urine or abdominal pain. Check with a clinician if you take diabetes, blood-pressure, sedative, seizure, thyroid or immunosuppressant medicines (NCCIH).

Evidence Level: Moderate

Several small RCTs suggest reduced stress and cortisol, and modest sleep benefits in people with insomnia. Evidence for testosterone and performance is preliminary.
