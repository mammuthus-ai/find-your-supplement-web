Vitamin D3 vs D2: Which Form Actually Works Better?

Wondering whether to take vitamin D3 or D2? Research shows D3 raises blood vitamin D levels more than D2 — about 40% more in daily-dose trials. This evidence-based guide breaks down the science, sensible doses, and when D2 still makes sense.

Two forms on the same shelf

Walk into any pharmacy and you'll find two versions of vitamin D on the shelf: D3 (cholecalciferol) and D2 (ergocalciferol). The bottles look nearly identical and cost about the same, but they are not quite interchangeable. If you take D2, a blood test will show whether it's working; D3 usually gets you there with a smaller dose.

What D3 and D2 actually are

Vitamin D isn't really a vitamin — it's a prohormone. UVB from sunlight converts a compound in your skin into vitamin D3; the liver turns that into 25-hydroxyvitamin D (what blood tests measure), and the kidneys make calcitriol — the active, hormone-like form that regulates calcium and many other processes.

  • Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol): animal-derived — fatty fish, egg yolks, liver — and the form your skin naturally produces. Most supplements source it from lanolin (sheep's wool); vegan versions come from lichen.
  • Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol): plant/fungal-derived, found in UV-exposed mushrooms and used to fortify some cereals and plant milks. Long used as a prescription-strength vitamin D.

Both raise blood levels. How efficiently is where the evidence diverges.

D3 raises blood levels more

A 2023 meta-analysis of 20 comparison studies found D2 raised total vitamin D about 40% less than D3 at the same daily dose (the gap was smaller or absent in people with overweight). An earlier 2012 meta-analysis in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition found D3 superior for large single doses, but not significantly different with daily dosing. Either form can correct a deficiency; with D2 you may simply need a somewhat higher dose.

Why the difference? Lab studies suggest the body processes and transports D3 somewhat more efficiently. One modeling study found D2's blood form is cleared faster, though it concluded that at typical treatment doses the clinical difference may be small.

Real-world records point the same way. A retrospective review of Qatari health records found a D3 injection raised levels more than a D2 injection (6.1 vs 3.2 ng/mL) — consistent with, but weaker evidence than, the randomized trials. For most people buying vitamin D over the counter, that makes D3 at a modest dose a sensible default.

When D2 is still the right choice

  • Vegans — with a caveat. D2 used to be the main plant-derived option, but vegan D3 from lichen is the same molecule as animal-derived D3. Look for a vegan lichen D3 of 1,000–2,000 IU rather than a 5,000 IU product.
  • Insurance-covered prescriptions. High-dose D2 (50,000 IU weekly) is a decades-old standard, often covered when over-the-counter D3 is not. If cost is the barrier, it's a legitimate choice — take it as prescribed and let your doctor decide whether to recheck your level.
  • Kidney disease. If you have kidney disease, your nephrologist should choose the form and dose.
  • Fortified foods. The D2 in cereals and plant milks is low-dose enough that the difference is a minor concern.

How much do you need?

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements RDA is 600 IU daily for ages 1–70 and 800 IU past 70, with an upper limit for adults of 4,000 IU per day. Most healthy adults need 600–800 IU (from food, sun and supplements); if a test shows deficiency, a clinician may advise 1,000–4,000 IU or a short higher-dose course. The Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline suggests against taking more than the recommended intake on your own if you are a healthy adult under 75.

One habit helps either form: take it with a meal that contains some fat — olive oil, avocado, nuts, fish. A 50-person single-dose study found 32% higher peak absorption with a meal containing fat.

Quick answers

My doctor prescribed D2 — should I switch?

Talk to your doctor before changing anything; prescription D2 is a well-established protocol. If you are on a maintenance dose, ask whether standard or vegan D3 is appropriate instead.

Do I need a blood test, and what level should I aim for?

Most people are sufficient at 20 ng/mL or above; testing is mainly for people with a medical reason, and your clinician can tell you your target. If you are retested after a dose change, levels shift gradually over weeks to months.

The bottom line

D3 is the evidence-supported default; D2 earns its place mainly for covered prescriptions and select clinical cases. Whichever you take, have it with a meal that contains fat and stay at or below 4,000 IU a day unless a clinician advises otherwise. Higher doses — and any supplementation with kidney disease, high blood calcium, or interacting medications, including thiazide diuretics, steroids, statins and orlistat — belong under a doctor's guidance.
