Hair Shedding in Women: What Actually Helps, and Why Supplements Rarely Do

Most hair loss in women comes from genetics or a recent trigger such as illness, childbirth or stress, not a missing vitamin. We check the evidence for iron, zinc, vitamin D, biotin and other popular hair supplements, explain why none is proven to regrow hair, and cover what helps instead: testing for real deficiencies, enough protein, time, and minoxidil for pattern thinning.

By the Find Your Supplement team · Published May 25, 2026 · Updated October 9, 2026

Most hair loss isn't a supplement problem

Noticing more strands in the shower drain, a wider part, or a thinning ponytail? The temptation is to grab whatever bottle promises hair, skin, and nails. Most are biotin gummies in a pink jar — and biotin is almost never why your hair is shedding.

The most common causes of hair loss in women are female pattern hair loss (a widening part or thinning crown) and telogen effluvium (extra shedding a few months after a trigger such as illness, childbirth, a crash diet or major stress), according to the NHS. Nutrition plays a supporting role: low iron, vitamin D or zinc, too little protein, and major stress are factors worth ruling out. Here is every popular option graded by evidence quality, plus a practical 90-day plan.

If your part is widening or your crown is thinning, start with the treatment that has real evidence: topical minoxidil is the standard first-line treatment for female pattern hair loss (NHS). Ask a pharmacist or doctor whether it suits you — it is not used in pregnancy or while breastfeeding. Supplements play a minor role at most.

Popular hair supplements: what the evidence really shows

No supplement is proven to regrow hair. A 2023 JAMA Dermatology systematic review found only small trials, several funded by the companies selling the product. The nutrients below are worth correcting only if a blood test shows you're low.

Iron — correct it if you are deficient (evidence for hair regrowth is limited). Low iron stores are easy to miss: your doctor can check serum ferritin, not just hemoglobin, because stores can be low while hemoglobin is normal. Menstruating women, vegetarians, blood donors, and endurance athletes are most at risk. The link to hair loss itself is debated: a 2022 meta-analysis of 36 studies found lower average ferritin in women with hair loss, but a 2010 Duke study of 457 women found iron deficiency was no more common in women with hair loss than in women without it. A 2017 review in Dermatology Practical & Conceptual and a 2019 review in Dermatology and Therapy both say a documented deficiency should be corrected, but there are no strong trials showing that iron regrows hair in people who have low ferritin without anemia. Some dermatologists aim for ferritin of at least 40 ng/mL, though this target isn't firmly established. Test first — too much iron is harmful. If a blood test shows low iron, your clinician may suggest about 25–45 mg of elemental iron every other day; alternate-day dosing is absorbed better than daily dosing, and 45 mg/day is the upper limit for adults unless your doctor prescribes more. A gentle form such as iron bisglycinate is a common choice. Keep iron out of children's reach — an accidental overdose can be fatal for a young child.

Vitamin D — people with hair loss tend to have lower levels, but trials showing that supplements regrow hair are few and low-quality. Hair follicles have vitamin D receptors, and a 2024 meta-analysis of observational studies found lower vitamin D levels in people with non-scarring alopecia. That is an association, not proof that supplements help: a 2023 JAMA Dermatology systematic review rated the vitamin D treatment evidence as low quality. According to the NIH, a blood level of 20 ng/mL or more is adequate for most people. If a test shows you're low, take what your clinician recommends, often 1,000–2,000 IU of vitamin D3 daily with a meal, and stay at or under 4,000 IU/day unless a doctor advises more.

Zinc — levels are lower on average in people with hair loss; replace it if a blood test is low. Hair loss is a known sign of zinc deficiency, and a 2013 study of 312 people with hair loss and 30 controls found lower average zinc in all four hair-loss types it looked at. It measured levels, though, not whether zinc treatment regrows hair. If you're low, 15–30 mg of zinc with food is typical; stay at or under the 40 mg/day upper limit from all sources — high zinc depletes copper.

Marine protein complexes — small, manufacturer-funded trials. Viviscal-type marine blends are among the few hair products tested in double-blind RCTs: a 2015 placebo-controlled trial in 60 women showed more terminal hairs and less shedding over 90 days. A smaller 2016 trial in men found similar results; both were funded by the manufacturer. A reasonable add-on once bloodwork has been checked; judge it at 3–6 months. Avoid if you have a fish or shellfish allergy.

Saw palmetto — limited evidence, mostly from small trials of mixed products in men. It may weakly inhibit 5-alpha-reductase, the enzyme that makes the androgen DHT. A 2020 systematic review found some improvement, but mostly in men using combination products (some applied to the scalp), and the authors said robust high-quality data are lacking. The NIH's NCCIH says the evidence is too limited to draw conclusions. There is little research in women. If you try it, the studied dose is 320 mg of standardized extract, and discuss it with your doctor first. Avoid it if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, trying to conceive, or on hormonal therapy without your doctor's sign-off.

Pumpkin seed oil and collagen — limited evidence. In one 76-man trial of a pumpkin-seed-oil-based combination supplement, 400 mg/day for 24 weeks raised mean hair count 40% versus 10% with placebo. In women, a small 2021 trial of pumpkin seed oil applied to the scalp (compared with minoxidil foam, no placebo group) showed some improvement — it tells us nothing about capsules. Cheap and generally safe, but thin data. Collagen is mostly a protein top-up — possibly helpful if you eat under ~0.8 g/kg/day of protein, redundant otherwise.

Biotin — insufficient evidence. Deficiency is rare, and outside true deficiency a 2017 review found no evidence it improves hair growth. Worse, high-dose biotin can interfere with lab tests — including troponin (used to diagnose heart attacks), TSH and other hormone tests — per FDA safety communications. Skip 5,000–10,000 mcg gummies; a multivitamin's 30–100 mcg is plenty, and tell your doctor before any blood test if you take biotin.

Caution: selenium and vitamin A. Excess harms — selenium toxicity directly causes hair loss (stay well under the 400 mcg/day upper limit, counting all supplements), and too much preformed vitamin A (above the 10,000 IU/day upper limit) is a known cause of shedding.

Pregnant or breastfeeding? Postpartum shedding is common and usually settles on its own. Check any supplement with your doctor or midwife first.

Which kind of hair loss do you have?

Supplements can only help certain mechanisms — pick the wrong target and you waste six months. Shedding that began 2–4 months after a trigger (postpartum, illness, crash diet, major stress) is most likely telogen effluvium, which usually recovers once the trigger has passed.

The practical 90-day plan

Step 1 — bloodwork before you buy anything. Ask your doctor about ferritin, 25-hydroxy vitamin D, TSH and free T4, and a CBC. Your doctor can tell you what counts as low for you.

Step 2 — protein and calories. Hair is a luxury tissue — your body sheds it first when it thinks you are starving, and crash diets often precede shedding. Aim for at least 1.0–1.2 g protein per kg daily, unless you have kidney disease.

Step 3 — supplement to your labs: low ferritin → iron as your clinician advises (often 25–45 mg every other day, with vitamin C); low vitamin D → often 1,000–2,000 IU D3 daily, no more than 4,000 IU without a doctor; low zinc → 15–30 mg; low protein → more protein-rich food first, or 10–20 g collagen or whey.

Step 4 — for pattern thinning, ask about minoxidil. Optional add-ons with small trials: a marine protein complex, or pumpkin seed oil 400 mg. Saw palmetto only after talking to your doctor.

Step 5 — patience. Shedding after a trigger usually slows within a few months, but the American Academy of Dermatology says hair can take 6–9 months to regain its normal fullness.

When to see a dermatologist

Skip the self-experiment if you have smooth bald patches, scalp pain or scaling, sudden rapid loss, shedding with fatigue or menstrual changes (think thyroid or PCOS), or over 12 months of progressive thinning. Some hair loss is medical, not nutritional — and untreated scarring alopecias become less reversible over time.

Bottom line

For most women with diffuse shedding, the highest-yield moves are finding and fixing any deficiency (iron and vitamin D are the usual suspects), eating enough protein, and time. For a widening part, minoxidil is the proven treatment. Several branded supplements have small positive trials, but none is proven (JAMA Dermatology review); saw palmetto and pumpkin seed oil have thin data, and biotin is mostly marketing unless you are deficient. Treat hair loss as a signal worth investigating, give the follicle cycle 6–9 months, and skip the biotin gummies.
