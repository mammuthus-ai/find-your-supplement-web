A 2012 meta-analysis of 19 RCTs found they cut hot flash frequency by 20.6% and severity by 26.2%, with products over 18.8 mg of genistein per day more than twice as effective (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22433977/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="font-semibold text-ink underline decoration-ember underline-offset-2">Taku et al., 2012</a>). But the effect is slow: a modelling analysis found soy isoflavones take about 13 weeks to reach even half their maximal effect and at least 48 weeks to reach 80% of it, with a maximum of about 25% beyond placebo (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25316502/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="font-semibold text-ink underline decoration-ember underline-offset-2">Li et al., Br J Clin Pharmacol, 2015</a>). The evidence is mixed, though: a 2022 meta-analysis of 11 trials (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35651836/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="font-semibold text-ink underline decoration-ember underline-offset-2">Kang et al., 2022</a>) and a 2025 meta-analysis of 12 trials (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40718787/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="font-semibold text-ink underline decoration-ember underline-offset-2">Luan et al., 2025</a>) found no significant effect on hot flashes. Give it at least 3 months and judge it honestly — any effect is modest and slow; whole-soy foods like tofu and edamame are an alternative. The Menopause Society does not recommend soy foods or extracts for hot flashes. Women with a history of estrogen-receptor-positive breast cancer should discuss concentrated soy extracts with their oncologist first.5:T51f,<strong>Melatonin</strong> rhythms flatten in menopausal women. A 2021 systematic review found supplementation improved subjective sleep quality in postmenopausal women with existing sleep problems, with doses of 3 mg and above also improving menopausal symptoms (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33969545/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="font-semibold text-ink underline decoration-ember underline-offset-2">Treister-Goltzman and Peleg, 2021</a>). Trials in menopausal women used about 3 mg, and the wider sleep evidence comes from 1–5 mg taken 1–3 hours before bed; pooled trials of doses under 1 mg found no clear effect. Expect a small benefit — a few minutes faster to fall asleep — not a cure. Melatonin labels are often inaccurate — one analysis found actual content ranged from 83% below to 478% above the label (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27855744/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="font-semibold text-ink underline decoration-ember underline-offset-2">Erland & Saxena, 2017</a>) — so pick a <a href="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=melatonin+3mg+usp+verified&tag=fysarticles-20" target="_blank" rel="sponsored noopener noreferrer" class="font-semibold text-ink underline decoration-ember underline-offset-2">USP-verified 3 mg product</a>.6:T607,<strong>Magnesium glycinate</strong>: a 2021 meta-analysis of three small, low-quality trials in older adults found magnesium shortened time to fall asleep by about 17 minutes, with no clear change in total sleep time (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33865376/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="font-semibold text-ink underline decoration-ember underline-offset-2">Mah and Pitre, 2021</a>), and a 2017 systematic review found suggestive but poor-quality evidence that magnesium eases mild anxiety (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28445426/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="font-semibold text-ink underline decoration-ember underline-offset-2">Boyle et al., 2017</a>).
