Creatine Is Not Just for Bodybuilders — What the Research Says About Your Brain

The most evidence-backed sports supplement also shows early, promising effects on memory and mood in some studies. Here's what the research says.

By the Find Your Supplement team · Published February 17, 2026 · Updated October 9, 2026

Beyond the Gym

Most people associate creatine with weight rooms and protein shakes. But creatine monohydrate is showing up in brain research more and more often. The findings are early, but some are worth knowing about.

What Is Creatine, Really?

Creatine is a naturally occurring compound found mainly in muscle, with smaller amounts in the brain. It's made mainly in the kidneys and liver from the amino acids arginine and glycine (with a methyl group from methionine), and also obtained from animal foods — which is why vegetarians and vegans have lower muscle creatine stores (brain levels appear similar, because the brain makes its own) (Solis et al., 2014).

Creatine's primary function is replenishing ATP (the cell's energy currency) during high-demand periods. In muscle, this means more reps and power. In the brain, creatine helps buffer energy supply. Whether supplements meaningfully improve thinking is still being studied.

The Cognitive Evidence

Sleep Deprivation

A small 2006 study (19 people taking 20 g/day for a week) found creatine reduced the decline in some mental tasks and mood after 24 hours without sleep (McMorris et al., 2006). That dose is four to seven times the 3–5 g/day most people take.

Vegetarians and Vegans

Plant-based diets contain essentially zero dietary creatine. A few small studies suggest vegetarians may benefit more. One 2011 trial found better memory in vegetarians but not meat-eaters (Benton and Donohoe, 2011), and a 2003 trial in vegetarians found better working-memory and reasoning scores (Rae et al., 2003).

A 2023 meta-analysis found a small memory benefit overall, seen mainly in older adults, with little effect in younger people (Prokopidis et al., 2023). A published letter has since questioned its statistics (Eckert and Pascher, 2023).

Aging and Neuroprotection

Creatine has been tested in Parkinson's disease and ALS, but large trials found no benefit. A trial of 1,741 people with Parkinson's taking 10 g/day was stopped early because it was not working (NET-PD investigators, 2015), and a Cochrane review of ALS trials found no difference in survival or disease progression (Pastula et al., 2012). Neuroprotection in people remains unproven.

Depression

A few small RCTs suggest adding creatine to standard depression treatment may speed improvement, for example a 2012 trial in 52 women on escitalopram (Lyoo et al., 2012). This is not a substitute for treatment; discuss it with your prescriber.

Dosing

  • Standard approach: 3–5 g/day of creatine monohydrate. No loading phase necessary.
  • Loading phase (optional): 20 g/day (split into four 5 g doses) for 5–7 days, then 3–5 g/day maintenance. Saturates muscle creatine stores faster but causes water retention.
  • Timing: Timing doesn't matter much. Consistency does.
  • Form: Creatine monohydrate is the best-studied and cheapest form. Don't overpay for so-called upgraded forms like creatine HCL or ethyl ester — monohydrate is the gold standard.

Note that some brain studies used higher doses (up to 20 g/day for short periods), and it isn't known whether 3–5 g/day raises brain creatine much.

Safety Profile

Creatine monohydrate is one of the most studied supplements in history. Decades of research show it is safe for healthy adults at standard doses (ISSN position stand, 2017). The main side effect is a small weight gain from water stored in muscle (not fat), mostly during loading.

Caution: Creatine can raise blood creatinine, a common kidney test, so tell your doctor you take it before blood work. Check with a clinician if you have kidney disease, take medicines that affect the kidneys, are pregnant, or are under 18.

Evidence Level: High for Exercise, Low to Moderate for the Brain

For exercise performance, creatine is backed by over 1,000 published studies, and the International Society of Sports Nutrition rates it as the most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement available (ISSN, 2017). For brain benefits, the evidence is small and mixed: promising, but not proven.
