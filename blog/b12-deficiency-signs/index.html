Vitamin B12 Deficiency Symptoms and Supplements: The Tingling, Fatigue, and Brain Fog You Keep Blaming on Everything Else

Vitamin B12 deficiency can hide behind ordinary tiredness until nerve symptoms start — and some nerve damage can become permanent if it goes untreated. This evidence-based guide covers the real symptoms, who is at risk, how it is diagnosed, when you need a doctor rather than a supplement, and which B12 form and dose the evidence supports.

By the Find Your Supplement team · Published June 20, 2026 · Updated October 31, 2026

The deficiency that masquerades as just getting older

You are exhausted in a way coffee no longer touches. Your hands and feet tingle, especially at night. Names slip away mid-sentence — you have quietly started calling it brain fog. Maybe your tongue feels sore and oddly smooth. Each symptom on its own is easy to wave away, which is why vitamin B12 deficiency is a nutritional problem that is easy to miss: the early symptoms are vague.

Why it matters: B12 deficiency can damage your nervous system, and some of that nerve damage can become permanent if untreated long enough. The neurological problems can also appear before — or without — the classic anemia on a routine blood test, so a normal blood count does not rule it out (Langan & Goodbred, AFP, 2017; NICE NG239). Caught in time, though, it is treatable: B12 replacement is standard care, any anemia corrects within weeks, and nerve symptoms usually improve over months, though they may only partly recover if the deficiency went on for long, and treatment trials have rarely measured symptoms at all.

The symptoms, from easy-to-miss to do-not-ignore

B12 (cobalamin) is the cofactor your body needs to build DNA, red blood cells, and the myelin sheath that insulates your nerves (Green et al., 2017). Because the liver stores several years' worth, deficiency develops slowly and silently.

  • Early and general: persistent fatigue that rest does not fix, pale or faintly lemon-tinted skin, palpitations and breathlessness on mild exertion, a sore, smooth, beefy-red tongue, loss of appetite
  • Neurological — the red flags: pins and needles or numbness in the hands and feet (often symmetrical), poor balance or unsteadiness in the dark, muscle weakness
  • Cognitive and mood: brain fog, word-finding trouble, memory problems, irritability and low mood

Clinical guidance stresses that neurological symptoms can appear without anemia — UK guidelines say not to rule out B12 deficiency just because there is no anemia, so do not wait for an abnormal blood count (AFP, 2017; NICE NG239).

Who is actually at risk

  • Vegans and long-term vegetarians — B12 occurs naturally almost exclusively in animal products. A review of 18 studies found deficiency common, and highest in vegans (Pawlak et al., 2013). If you eat plant-based, take a B12 supplement or reliably eat B12-fortified foods.
  • Adults over 50 — stomach acid drops with age, impairing release of B12 from food. Depending on the cutoff used, an estimated 3% to 43% of older adults living in the community are deficient (NIH ODS, 2024 version).
  • Metformin users — in a randomized trial in people with insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, metformin taken for about 4 years caused a mean 19% drop in B12 and about 7 percentage points more outright deficiency than placebo (de Jager et al., BMJ, 2010).
  • Long-term PPI or H2-blocker users — less acid to free B12 from food.
  • Pernicious anemia, gut surgery, Crohn's, or celiac disease — these need lifelong treatment under a doctor. UK guidelines recommend lifelong injections for pernicious anemia and after total gastrectomy or removal of the end of the small intestine; for celiac, Crohn's or bariatric surgery, at least 1,000 mcg orally per day or injections, as your clinician advises (NICE NG239).

Getting tested properly

Serum B12 below ~150 pg/mL indicates deficiency, but the test is famously blunt: many symptomatic people sit in a low-normal gray zone around 150–300 pg/mL. The tiebreaker is methylmalonic acid (MMA), with greater than 95% sensitivity for true deficiency (AFP, 2017) — though MMA can be falsely high with kidney disease. If yours is low-normal and symptoms fit, ask your clinician about an MMA test before concluding you are fine. And if you suspect deficiency, get blood tests before starting a high-dose supplement, since it can hide the diagnosis (NICE NG239).

For the vast majority of people, any of these forms works — there is no evidence that absorption differs by form, and trials proving methylcobalamin clinically superior are lacking, so cyanocobalamin's track record, stability, and low cost make it the sensible default (AFP, 2017; NIH ODS, 2024 version). If you have kidney disease, ask your nephrologist which form to use. Compare cyanocobalamin B12 and methylcobalamin B12.

How much to take — and when oral B12 is a reasonable option instead of injections

The RDA is just 2.4 mcg/day for adults, but that only prevents deficiency when absorption is normal. Correcting one requires far more, because only about 1% of a large oral dose is absorbed — roughly 1.3% of a 1,000 mcg dose (NIH ODS, 2024 version).

  • Maintenance: a daily supplement (many contain 25–250 mcg) or B12-fortified foods; adults over 50 and vegans are advised to use fortified foods or supplements (AFP, 2017)
  • Correcting a diagnosed deficiency: research suggests 1,000 mcg orally per day, under your clinician's guidance

A Cochrane review of three small trials (153 people) found low-quality evidence that high-dose oral B12 raises blood levels about as well as injections, at lower cost; none of the trials measured symptoms (Wang et al., Cochrane, 2018). Get prompt medical treatment — not just a supplement — if you have numbness, balance problems or memory changes; injections are standard for pernicious anemia and for significant neurological symptoms, and treatment should not be delayed while waiting for test results (NICE NG239). B12 has no established upper limit because it is considered safe even at high doses (NIH ODS). A 1,000 mcg B12 covers oral repletion; evidence suggests sublingual versions work no better than swallowed tablets (NIH ODS, 2024 version). And if your B12 is normal, extra B12 has not been shown to boost energy or performance — any benefit comes from fixing a real shortfall.

The bottom line

Fatigue, tingling hands and feet, brain fog, a sore smooth tongue, and balance problems are the classic cluster — and they can appear before or without anemia. Know your risk group, test properly (serum B12, then MMA if low-normal) before you start high-dose B12, default to cheap cyanocobalamin, and know that high-dose oral B12 is a reasonable option for many people — but pernicious anemia and nerve symptoms need a doctor and often injections. Act early: some nerve damage from prolonged deficiency is permanent. This is not medical advice — talk to a healthcare provider before starting or changing supplements, especially if you are pregnant, on metformin or acid blockers, or have a diagnosed condition.
