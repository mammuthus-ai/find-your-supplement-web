Supplements for Energy: What Actually Helps When You're Tired (and When Nothing Will)

Most tiredness comes from sleep, stress, mood, and everyday life, not a missing nutrient. This evidence-based guide covers when B12, iron, vitamin D, magnesium, CoQ10, and ashwagandha can actually help, which blood tests to ask about first, and what the studies really show.

Why you're tired: usually not a supplement problem

Feeling tired usually has an ordinary cause: too little sleep, stress, low mood, an illness, or a medicine. In a Dutch study of 571 people who saw their family doctor about tiredness, 65 percent reported sleep problems and 61 percent reported distress or worry, while only about 8 percent were found to have a clear physical illness within a year. Anemia turned up in 9 people and low B12 in just 1 (Nijrolder et al., 2009). Doctors are advised to look at sleep, lifestyle, mood, and medicines first (Latimer et al., 2023; NHS).

Sometimes, though, it really is a nutrient you're low on. Some deficiencies, especially iron and vitamin B12, can cause anemia, which means less oxygen reaches your muscles and brain. That's a real, testable cause of tiredness. The supplements below can help when you're actually low in them, so testing comes first. If your levels are fine, there's no good evidence that extra B12 boosts energy or endurance (NIH ODS), and in one trial in tired women, iron helped only those with low iron stores (Verdon et al., 2003).

If you've been tired for weeks without a clear reason, see your doctor. Common first blood tests include a full blood count (for anemia), ferritin (your iron stores), thyroid function, and blood sugar (HbA1c), with B12 added if your doctor thinks it's relevant (NICE's ME/CFS guideline, NG206; Latimer et al., 2023). Chronic stress can wreck sleep and leave you drained, but despite the internet, "adrenal fatigue" isn't a real diagnosis (Cadegiani and Kater, 2016). And caffeine doesn't add energy. It hides sleepiness by blocking a brain signal called adenosine, and too much, or too late in the day, can cut into your sleep, even when taken 6 hours before bed (Drake et al., 2013).

1. Vitamin B12: a common, testable cause of tiredness

B12 is essential for red blood cell formation, DNA synthesis, and nerve function. Without enough B12, your bone marrow can't build red blood cells properly. It makes fewer, oversized, faulty cells (megaloblastic anemia), so less oxygen reaches your body and you can feel tired and weak, sometimes severely (NHS). Low B12 also affects nerves. Left untreated for too long, it can cause nerve damage that sometimes doesn't fully heal.

It's easy to miss: the body stores years' worth of B12, so symptoms creep up slowly and look like many other things (Langan and Goodbred, 2017). The standard blood test isn't perfect either. Results can land in a borderline zone, and doctors can then order a follow-up test (methylmalonic acid, or "active B12") (NICE NG239). Reviews of studies in vegetarians and vegans have found B12 deficiency rates anywhere from near zero to over 80 percent, depending on the group and whether people took supplements, and vegans who don't supplement are at the highest risk (Pawlak et al., 2013; Pawlak et al., 2014).

  • At risk: vegans and vegetarians who don't take B12 or eat fortified foods (B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods), older adults (risk climbs with age), long-term users of metformin or acid-reducing medicines (PPIs, H2 blockers), and people with pernicious anemia, celiac or Crohn's disease, or past stomach or bowel surgery, including weight-loss surgery (NIH ODS; NICE NG239).
  • Test before you take it: if you think you're low, get a blood test before starting B12 pills, because supplements can make the test look fine even when the problem isn't fixed (NICE NG239).
  • Form matters less than dose: NICE lists cyanocobalamin and methylcobalamin as acceptable forms (NICE NG239). Most trials, including the dose studies below, used cyanocobalamin, which is usually the cheaper option.
  • Dose: to prevent deficiency (for example, if you're vegan), about 50 mcg a day, or a larger weekly dose, works for most people who absorb B12 normally (Del Bo' et al., 2019). Older adults who are mildly deficient needed about 650 to 1,000 mcg a day to correct it in one trial (Eussen et al., 2005), and people with absorption problems are usually prescribed at least 1,000 mcg a day (NICE NG239). If a doctor confirms you're deficient, treatment is often 1,000 to 2,000 mcg a day by mouth, but some causes, like pernicious anemia, need injections. Get the cause diagnosed rather than self-treating.

2. Vitamin D: only helps if you're actually low

Your body turns vitamin D into a hormone that switches hundreds of genes on or off. Low vitamin D and tiredness often show up together, but studies disagree on whether one causes the other. A study of genetic data from over 300,000 people found little evidence that low vitamin D causes tiredness (Havdahl et al., 2019), and a trial in people with chronic fatigue syndrome found high-dose vitamin D did not improve fatigue (Witham et al., 2015).

The best positive evidence is small. A 2016 trial in 120 young adults who were truly deficient (below 20 ng/mL), and who had other causes of tiredness ruled out, found a single vitamin D dose modestly reduced tiredness after 4 weeks (Nowak et al., 2016). Large trials in mostly healthy older adults with normal levels found 2,000 IU a day didn't improve physical performance, falls, or frailty (LeBoff et al., 2023). In the US, about 1 in 20 people are at risk of true deficiency and nearly 1 in 4 are below the 20 ng/mL level experts call adequate (Herrick et al., 2019). Rates are higher in winter, in people with darker skin, in people who live far from the equator, and in people who spend most of the day indoors (NIH ODS).

Dose: most adults need 600 to 800 IU a day in total, from food, sun, or a 400 IU supplement. If a blood test shows you're low, your doctor may suggest more for a while. Don't go above 4,000 IU a day, the upper limit for adults, without medical advice (NIH ODS). Testing isn't routinely recommended for healthy adults (Demay et al., 2024). If your doctor checks it, 20 ng/mL or above is considered adequate for most people.

3. Iron: test first, then find the cause

Iron is central to hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body. Run low, and you make less of it, which can cause the fatigue, weakness, and shortness of breath on exertion of iron deficiency anemia (NIH ODS; WHO). Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional cause of anemia worldwide (WHO). It's most likely in women with heavy periods, pregnant women, frequent blood donors, endurance athletes (particularly women), vegetarians and vegans, and people with celiac disease or IBD. Plant (non-heme) iron is absorbed much less well than the heme iron in meat, roughly 2 to 20 percent versus 15 to 35 percent (Abbaspour et al., 2014).

Low iron stores can matter even without anemia. In one trial of 198 menstruating women who felt tired and had low iron stores (ferritin below 50) but no anemia, those who took iron for 12 weeks reported a bigger drop in fatigue than those on a placebo, about 48 percent versus 29 percent (Vaucher et al., 2012). Across trials, iron reduced how tired people felt but didn't improve measured exercise capacity (Houston et al., 2018). That's in people whose blood test showed low iron. It won't help if your iron is already fine.

Important: get a blood test first: a full blood count plus ferritin (your iron stores), and transferrin saturation if you have an inflammatory condition. Too much iron is harmful. High doses upset the stomach, very high doses can be dangerous, and the adult upper limit is 45 mg a day unless a doctor prescribes more (NIH ODS). If a test shows low iron, your doctor should also look for the cause, such as heavy periods, gut bleeding, or celiac disease, rather than just topping you up (AGA). If you are low, a gentler form such as ferrous bisglycinate may cause fewer stomach side effects than ferrous sulfate. The evidence is strongest in pregnancy and comes from small trials (Fischer et al., 2023). Your doctor will choose the dose.

4. Magnesium: needed for energy, but deficiency is uncommon

Most ATP, the cell's energy currency, works as a complex bound to magnesium, so the many reactions that use ATP depend on it. Real magnesium deficiency can impair energy production, but it is uncommon in healthy people (NIH ODS). About half of Americans get less magnesium from food than they need on average (48 percent in national survey data), though low intake isn't the same as deficiency (Rosanoff et al., 2012). Early signs of deficiency are vague (tiredness, weakness, poor appetite, nausea), and cramps and twitching come later. These symptoms have many other causes. A normal blood magnesium test doesn't rule out low body stores, and magnesium isn't part of a routine tiredness workup. Small studies in older adults with insomnia suggest magnesium may help sleep a little, but the evidence is weak (Mah and Pitre, 2021).

Dose: typical supplement doses are 200 to 350 mg of elemental magnesium a day. Don't go above 350 mg from supplements unless a doctor advises it, because higher doses commonly cause diarrhea and cramping (NIH ODS). Avoid magnesium supplements if you have kidney disease unless your doctor says otherwise. Forms that dissolve well, such as magnesium citrate, tend to be absorbed better than magnesium oxide. There's no good evidence that any one form, including malate, boosts energy (Russell et al., 1995).

5. CoQ10: modest evidence, mostly in people with health conditions

CoQ10 sits in the inner membrane of mitochondria and plays an essential role in the electron transport chain, which makes most of the body's ATP. CoQ10 levels in several organs appear to fall after early adulthood, based on small autopsy studies, but whether this causes tiredness, or whether supplements reverse it, is not established (Kalén et al., 1989). Statins lower blood CoQ10 levels because they block a pathway that makes both cholesterol and CoQ10 (Banach et al., 2015). Individual trials vary, but overall the evidence doesn't show CoQ10 relieves statin-related muscle aches (NCCIH). Don't stop or change a statin without your doctor.

A 2022 meta-analysis of 13 small trials (about 1,100 people, mostly with conditions such as fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, MS, or heart failure) found CoQ10 modestly reduced fatigue scores. The benefit in healthy people was not statistically clear, and the authors flagged possible publication bias (Tsai et al., 2022).

Dose: trials in that review used about 60 to 500 mg a day, most often 100 to 300 mg, taken with a meal that contains fat (Tsai et al., 2022). CoQ10 comes as ubiquinone or ubiquinol, and the body converts between them. An oil-based softgel seems to matter more for absorption than the form (López-Lluch et al., 2019). Ubiquinol costs more, and the evidence that it works better, at any age, is limited. Safety: CoQ10 may interact with warfarin (a blood thinner) and insulin. Check with your doctor or pharmacist first if you take these or are having cancer treatment (NCCIH).

6. Ashwagandha: some evidence for stress, less for fatigue

Rather than fixing a deficiency, ashwagandha is studied for the stress response. Several small trials, mostly in stressed adults in India and often using product supplied by the extract maker, found the KSM-66 extract lowered self-reported stress (Chandrasekhar et al., 2012; Salve et al., 2019). In those trials, morning cortisol fell roughly 15 to 30 percent versus placebo at 250 to 600 mg a day, but cortisol is a lab marker, and lower cortisol doesn't automatically mean more energy. Evidence for fatigue specifically is thinner. A 2021 trial in 50 athletic adults found 600 mg a day for 8 weeks modestly improved VO2max and self-rated recovery and energy versus placebo. It was a single small study, and the results may not apply to non-athletes (Tiwari et al., 2021). One small trial in men starting weight training found smaller rises in a muscle-damage marker (creatine kinase) (Wankhede et al., 2015). Some people may feel less stressed on ashwagandha (NCCIH).

Dose: the trials used 250 to 600 mg a day of KSM-66 extract, so check the label names KSM-66 and the dose matches. Studies lasted about 2 to 3 months, and long-term safety is unknown. If you use it, stop after about 3 months and review with your clinician.

Safety: not for use if you're pregnant or breastfeeding, or have thyroid or autoimmune disease or liver problems. Rare cases of liver injury have been reported (LiverTox). Stop and see a doctor if you notice yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, or itching. It may interact with thyroid, diabetes, blood-pressure, sedative, and immune-suppressing drugs (NCCIH). Denmark's food safety agency has banned its sale in food and supplements, citing possible effects on hormones (Danish Veterinary and Food Administration, in Danish).

How to choose what's right for you

The right supplement, if any, depends on your root cause, and many causes of fatigue need a doctor, not a supplement:

  • Dietary gap (vegan, vegetarian, restricted eating): ask about B12 and ferritin tests. B12 is worth taking anyway if you're vegan.
  • Chronic stress: sleep and stress management first. Ashwagandha has some small-trial support for stress. Evidence that magnesium eases stress or anxiety is weak (Boyle et al., 2017).
  • New tiredness at any age: see a clinician first (anemia, thyroid, sleep apnea, medication side effects). Vitamin D helps only if a test shows you're low. Evidence for CoQ10 in otherwise healthy older adults is limited.
  • Tired with exercise: get ferritin checked. Iron helps if you're low, especially menstruating women and endurance athletes. Creatine can improve repeated high-intensity efforts, but it isn't an energy fix (Kreider et al., 2017).

What doesn't work (despite the marketing)

High-dose B vitamins: if you're not deficient, there's little good evidence that they boost energy. Megadoses aren't always harmless either: long-term high-dose B6 can damage nerves (NHS). Ginseng may give a small reduction in fatigue, but the studies are low quality and results conflict. Its most common side effect is insomnia, and it may affect blood clotting and blood sugar, so check with your doctor if you take blood thinners or diabetes medicine (NCCIH). Ginseng products also vary widely in their active compounds, with up to 36-fold differences in one analysis, so a store product may not match what was studied (Harkey et al., 2001). Proprietary energy blends only have to list the blend's total weight, so you often can't tell how much caffeine or herbal stimulant is in each serving (21 CFR 101.36). If caffeine is what works for you, coffee or tea is cheaper and you know the dose.

The foundation of steady energy is still enough sleep, regular movement, managing stress, and finding any real deficiency. Supplements are the last layer. When they correct a confirmed deficiency, they can help. Otherwise they usually do little. Talk to a healthcare provider before supplementing, particularly for iron, ashwagandha, or if you take prescription medicines.
